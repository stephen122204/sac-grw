\begin{abstract}
Gradient random-walk methods represent the spatial derivative of a scalar
solution by signed particles and recover the field by cumulative summation.
When such a field is estimated by averaging repeated simulations, the accuracy
of the average depends on how the random increments of the simulations are
coupled. At a fixed diffusion stage, reflecting the Gaussian increments
maximizes a matched pair's conditional variance contribution when its masses
have opposite signs. We therefore reflect
matched increments when the masses agree in sign and synchronize them when they
do not. For a single matched pair this rule minimizes the covariance of the two
contributions pointwise, and it leaves the discrete law of each simulation
exactly unchanged, so the average retains the bias of the underlying solver. We
derive an exact expression for the resulting reduction of conditional
integrated variance at one diffusion stage, and we identify the configurations
in which the rule provably does nothing. Numerical experiments on viscous
Burgers and cubic-flux problems compare the coupling with reflected pairing,
with pairing restricted to each sign class, with conditional expectation and
control variates, and with a randomized quasi-Monte Carlo adaptation. Against
within-sign pairing the integrated field variance falls to
$0.739\,[0.682,0.801]$ of its value at comparable cost, and in an experiment
with plans selected before evaluation the coupled estimator reaches a
prescribed expected-error target using four solver trajectories against six.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Monte Carlo methods offer a way to approximate solutions of partial
differential equations through averages of simulated trajectories. With
independent samples and finite variance, the standard error decays as the
inverse square root of the sample count. The variance and the cost of a sample
still depend on the problem, so reducing either can make a substantial
difference to the work needed for a given accuracy. Among the Monte Carlo
methods developed for one-dimensional
parabolic and viscous problems, one family replaces the solution by its spatial
derivative and carries that derivative on signed particles
\citep{ghoniem1985,shermanpeskin1986,shermanpeskin1988,roberts1989,shermanmascagni1994}.
The field is then recovered as a cumulative sum of particle masses rather than
as a histogram. Because particles accumulate where the derivative is large, the
representation resolves fronts and steep layers automatically, and the
reconstruction requires no grid. These gradient random-walk methods have been
analyzed for viscous scalar conservation laws
\citep{roberts1989,bossytalay1997,bossy2004,bossyjourdain2002,jourdain2000,bencheikh2020,towers2025},
and have recently been extended by Bertaglia, Pareschi and
Caflisch~\citep{bertaglia2024} to relaxation approximations of hyperbolic
conservation laws, including systems.

We are concerned with a question that arises once such a solver has been
chosen. A single simulation returns a field with sampling error; the usual
remedy is to average several independent simulations, at a cost proportional to
their number. Antithetic sampling seeks to improve the accuracy obtained from
the same number of simulations: run two
simulations whose random inputs are negatively related, so that their errors
partly cancel in the average, while each simulation individually retains its
correct law \citep{hammersley1956,glasserman2004}. For a particle solver the
natural implementation is immediate. The solver already sorts its particles by
position at every step, so the two simulations can be matched at equal spatial
rank, and the matched Gaussian diffusion increments can be reflected,
$Z\mapsto-Z$.

The signed representation gives us a reason to reconsider that choice at each
matched pair. Reflecting the increments of two matched particles
makes their positions move in opposite directions. In a cumulative
reconstruction, a particle contributes its mass to the field to the right of
its position, so moving two particles apart moves their contributions apart
only if the two masses have the same sign. If the matched masses have opposite
signs, the same reflection makes their contributions to the field move
\emph{together}, and the covariance that antithetic sampling is meant to make
negative becomes positive instead. Section~\ref{sec:motivation} makes this
precise: at a fixed pre-stage state, reflection minimizes the covariance of the two
contributions when the masses agree in sign and maximizes it when they do not,
and in each case the extremum is attained over all couplings of the two
increments of one matched pair. This suggests a simple modification. We keep the matching by
spatial rank and let the mass signs choose the orientation of the increment,
reflecting when the signs agree and synchronizing when they differ.

There is another way to use the mass signs: keep reflecting every matched pair,
but restrict the matching so
that particles are only ever paired with particles of the same sign. This
within-sign rule never creates an unfavorable pair. It also gives up the
spatial matching, since the $r$th positive particle of one simulation need not
lie near the $r$th positive particle of the other. Which construction is better is not
decidable by inspection, and the comparison between the two is the central
empirical question of this paper. Reflected pairing enters as the common
baseline against which both are measured.

The construction builds on established coupling ideas. Nüsken and
Pavliotis~\citep{nusken2018} develop a general
framework for coupling copies of a sampler while preserving marginals, and show
that the efficient coupling switches between reflected and synchronous noise
according to the sign of a product; their Proposition~56, Eq.~(141), makes the switch
depend on $\varphi'(x)\varphi'(y)$, where $\varphi$ solves a Poisson equation
associated with the observable, and their Corollary~60, Eq.~(144), gives a
Poisson-free special case in which the switch depends on $x\cdot y$ alone. Their
Section~5.2, Eqs.~(159)--(160), sorts the copies at every step, by $|\nabla\varphi|$, and couples
adjacent members of the sorted list. Their analysis concerns the asymptotic
variance of an ergodic, non-interacting sampler, and does not supply a
finite-time comparison for the interacting solver considered here, but the
switching principle and the repeated sorted matching are theirs. What is
specific to the present setting is the choice of keys: the sort key is the
particle position, which the solver computes anyway for the reconstruction, and
the switch key is the gradient mass the particle already carries.

Within the gradient-particle literature, Lécot~\citep{lecot_rims2001} combines
signed cumulative reconstruction, re-sorting of the particles at every step,
and quasi-random inputs to the diffusion stage for a Burgers problem; a related
treatment appears in Lécot and Ogawa~\citep{lecot2002}. This is the closest
precedent for structuring the random inputs of this class of solver, and our
quasi-Monte Carlo comparison adapts his rank-and-increment map rather than
reproducing his complete algorithm. Antithetic ideas have also been applied to
mean-field particle systems by Bencheikh and
Jourdain~\citep{bencheikhjourdain2019}, who couple a $2N$-particle system with
two $N$-particle systems in order to cancel a particle-number bias. That
construction compares systems of different sizes, whereas we average two
systems of the same size, each carrying the same bias. Recent quasi-Monte Carlo
constructions for McKean--Vlasov dynamics \citep{benrached2024} and array-RQMC
for a Fokker--Planck particle model \citep{netterdon2026} couple randomness
within an interacting ensemble under assumptions different from ours.

The paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:background} recalls the
signed gradient-particle discretization, fixes the update whose law we shall
preserve, and defines the estimators and the error functional.
Section~\ref{sec:coupling} develops the coupling: the pairwise calculation that
dictates the switching rule, the admissible class in which all three pairings
sit, the preservation of each simulation's law and its consequence for the
bias, an exact expression for the variance reduction obtained at one diffusion
stage, and the configurations in which the construction provably does nothing.
Section~\ref{sec:numerics} reports the numerical comparisons: against the two
alternative pairings, against conditional expectation and control variates
applied to the same solver, across initial profiles, observation times and
fluxes, at prescribed accuracy targets with plans frozen before evaluation, and
for nonlinear functionals and the regime in which discretization bias dominates.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} concludes. The proofs, the test problems and
reference solutions, the supporting estimators, the statistical methodology and
the evidence provenance are collected in the appendices.

Throughout, the computations are one-dimensional and scalar, the particle
masses are fixed at initialization, and transport uses the reconstructed
velocity rather than a sampled velocity label. We compare estimators within
this solver and do not compare the solver with deterministic alternatives.

\section{Signed Gradient Particles for Viscous Conservation Laws}
\label{sec:background}

This section fixes the discretization whose law the coupling of
Section~\ref{sec:coupling} will preserve. Nothing in it is new; we state it in
full because the coupling rule, the law-preservation argument, and the variance
identity all refer to specific details of the update.

\subsection{From the Field to its Gradient}
\label{sec:representation}

We consider the scalar viscous conservation law
\begin{equation}
 \partial_t u+\partial_x f(u)=\nu\,\partial_{xx}u,\qquad
 u(x,0)=u_0(x),\quad x\in\R,\quad 0<t\le T,
 \label{eq:pde}
\end{equation}
with $f\in C^1(\R)$ and $\nu>0$. The initial datum $u_0$ has bounded variation
and integrable derivative, and the limits
$u_\pm=\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}u_0(x)$ exist. Our experiments use
$f(u)=u^2/2$ (viscous Burgers), $f(u)=u^3/3$, and $f\equiv0$; the last is the
heat equation and serves as a control in which the coupling acts in the absence
of transport.

Let $w=\partial_x u$. Differentiating \eqref{eq:pde} in $x$ for a smooth
solution and using $\partial_x f(u)=f'(u)\,\partial_x u=f'(u)w$ gives
\begin{equation}
 \partial_t w+\partial_x\bigl(f'(u)\,w\bigr)=\nu\,\partial_{xx}w,
 \qquad w(x,0)=u_0'(x).
 \label{eq:gradient-pde}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:gradient-pde} is a conservation law for $w$ with velocity
field $f'(u)$ and diffusivity $\nu$: the gradient is advected at the local
characteristic speed and spread by diffusion. It is therefore natural to
approximate $w$ by point masses that are transported and diffused, which is the
idea underlying gradient random-walk methods
\citep{ghoniem1985,roberts1989,shermanmascagni1994}.

Accordingly we represent $w$ by $N$ signed particles with positions $X_i$ and
fixed masses $m_i$, and recover $u$ by integrating $w$ from the left,
\begin{equation}
 w^N(\cdot,t)=\sum_{i=1}^N m_i\,\delta_{X_i(t)},
 \qquad
 U^N(x,t)=u_-+\!\!\sum_{i:\,X_i(t)\le x}\!\! m_i ,
 \label{eq:representation}
\end{equation}
where $\delta_{X_i}$ is the Dirac measure at $X_i$. The second expression in
\eqref{eq:representation} is the cumulative sum that gives the method its
character: every particle to the left of $x$ contributes its mass to the field
at $x$, so the reconstruction is a signed step function whose jumps sit at the
particle positions. It is the discrete analog of $u=u_-+\int_{-\infty}^{x}w$,
and the same construction, with $\pm1$ masses and a left reconstruction,
appears in Lécot~\citep[Eqs. (35)--(37)]{lecot_rims2001}.

Two consequences of \eqref{eq:representation} are used repeatedly. First,
letting $x\to+\infty$ gives
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{i=1}^N m_i \;=\; u_+-u_- ,
 \label{eq:total-mass}
\end{equation}
so the total signed mass is fixed by the far-field data, exactly as in
Lécot~\citep[Eq. (36)]{lecot_rims2001}. For every profile with equal far-field
values this total vanishes. Second, the response of $U^N(x,\cdot)$ to a
displacement of particle $i$ has the sign of $m_i$: moving a positive-mass
particle to the right removes mass from the field to its left, while moving a
negative-mass particle to the right adds field there. This elementary
observation is what makes the sign of a mass, and not only the position of a
particle, relevant to the design of a coupling.

We initialize deterministically, so that both simulations of a pair start from
an identical state. For the main experiments we place $N/2$ particles at
midpoint quantiles of the positive part of $u_0'$ and $N/2$ at those of the
negative part and assign magnitude
\begin{equation}
 |m_i|=\frac{\mathrm{TV}(u_0)}{N},\qquad
 \mathrm{TV}(u_0)=\int_\R |u_0'(x)|\,\mathrm dx .
 \label{eq:mass}
\end{equation}
Equal counts and equal magnitudes then give $\sum_i m_i=0$ exactly. The
profile/time study of Section~\ref{sec:profiles} instead normalizes each sign
class by its own sampled integral, and the one-sign control of
Section~\ref{sec:structure} has all masses of one sign; both constructions are
specified in Appendix~\ref{app:setup}. We flag in Section~\ref{sec:identity}
the one place where a vanishing total mass is used.

\subsection{One Step of a Simulation}
\label{sec:onestep}

Fix a time step $h>0$, let $K=T/h\in\mathbb N$, and set
\begin{equation}
 \sigma=\sqrt{2\nu h},
 \label{eq:sigma}
\end{equation}
the standard deviation of the Brownian displacement accumulated over one step
by the diffusion in \eqref{eq:gradient-pde} \citep{ghoniem1985}. A simulation
carries the state $(X,m,v)$ of positions, masses, and the velocities to be used
by the next transport. One step is the operator splitting of
\eqref{eq:gradient-pde} into transport and diffusion, given as
Algorithm~\ref{alg:single}.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{One step of a single simulation.}
\label{alg:single}
\SetAlgoLined
\KwIn{state $(X,m,v)$, step $h$, viscosity $\nu$, flux derivative $f'$}
transport: $X_i \leftarrow X_i + v_i\,h$ for $i=1,\dots,N$\;
sort: $\pi\leftarrow$ a stable sort of $X$, \quad $(X,m)\leftarrow(X_\pi,m_\pi)$\;
velocity: $v_i \leftarrow f'\!\bigl(u_- + \sum_{j\le i} m_j\bigr)$\;
diffusion: draw $Z\sim\mathcal N(0,I_N)$ independent of the past, \quad
  $X_i \leftarrow X_i + \sigma Z_i$\;
\end{algorithm}

The velocity step deserves comment, since it is where \eqref{eq:representation}
re-enters. After the sort, the cumulative sum $u_-+\sum_{j\le i}m_j$ is exactly
$U^N$ evaluated at the position of particle $i$, inclusive of that particle's
own mass; the velocity assigned to particle $i$ is therefore $f'$ of the
reconstructed field at that particle. Because the reconstruction is a step
function, an inclusive and an exclusive convention differ by one mass, and we
record that we use the inclusive one at every rank and in every coupling. This
is a mean-transport update: the velocity is read off the reconstruction rather
than sampled from a two-speed equilibrium, and we make no claim here about the
relative merits of the two choices.

Three further details are used later. The velocity computed at step~3 is not
consumed until the transport of the following step, so it is part of the state
and must travel with its particle under any reordering. The sort is stable, so
coincident particles keep their previous relative order. Finally, the positions
are not re-sorted between the diffusion of one step and the transport of the
next; the sort at step~2 does that work.

\subsection{Estimators and Error}
\label{sec:estimators}

Write $U^{(A)},U^{(B)}$ for the fields reconstructed from two simulations at
time $T$. Averaging $B$ independent simulations gives the unpaired estimator
\begin{equation}
 \hat u^{\mathrm{sing}}_B(x)=\frac1B\sum_{b=1}^{B}U^{(b)}(x).
 \label{eq:est-single}
\end{equation}
When the simulations are run in coupled pairs we average within a pair first,
\begin{equation}
 \bar U^{(b)}(x)=\tfrac12\bigl(U^{(A,b)}(x)+U^{(B,b)}(x)\bigr),
 \label{eq:est-pair}
\end{equation}
and then over $B$ independent realizations of the pair,
\begin{equation}
 \hat u_B(x)=\frac1B\sum_{b=1}^{B}\bar U^{(b)}(x).
 \label{eq:est-ens}
\end{equation}
Thus $B$ counts simulations in \eqref{eq:est-single} and pairs in
\eqref{eq:est-ens}, and a paired estimate at a given $B$ costs $2B$ solver
trajectories. We report trajectory counts explicitly wherever the two
conventions meet.

Accuracy is measured by the expected integrated squared error on a window
$\Omega=[-5,5]$, discretized at $L=400$ equispaced points $x_\ell$ with spacing
$\Delta x$,
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal E(B)=\E\bigl\|\hat u_B-u_{\mathrm{ref}}\bigr\|_\Omega^2,
 \qquad
 \|g\|_\Omega^2=\Delta x\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}g(x_\ell)^2 ,
 \label{eq:error}
\end{equation}
with $u_{\mathrm{ref}}$ an independently computed spectral or Cole--Hopf
solution (Appendix~\ref{app:setup}). Since the $B$ realizations are independent
and identically distributed, \eqref{eq:error} splits as
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal E(B)=b^2+\frac{V}{B},
 \qquad
 b^2=\bigl\|\E\bar U-u_{\mathrm{ref}}\bigr\|_\Omega^2,
 \qquad
 V=\Delta x\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\Var\bigl(\bar U(x_\ell)\bigr).
 \label{eq:decomposition}
\end{equation}
The squared bias $b^2$ collects the finite-particle and time-stepping errors of
the underlying discretization, together with any error in the reference; the
integrated variance $V$ is the only term that responds to averaging more
realizations at fixed $N$ and $h$. A coupling can act on $V$ alone. This is not
a limitation peculiar to the present construction, but it does fix what any
comparison between couplings can be about, and Section~\ref{sec:biasregime}
returns to the regime in which $b^2$ dominates.

Computational work is measured as elapsed execution time $W=BC$, with $C$ the
measured cost of one realization; each experiment states its timing protocol,
and the cost of selecting a plan from preliminary runs is always reported
separately from the cost of executing it.

\begin{remark}[whole line versus window]
\label{rem:window}
The norm in \eqref{eq:error} is a quadrature on the bounded window $\Omega$ and
is used for every numerical comparison. The identity of
Section~\ref{sec:identity} instead integrates over all of $\R$. We keep the two
apart and never substitute one for the other.
\end{remark}

\section{Sign-Aware Coupling of Two Simulations}
\label{sec:coupling}

\subsection{What the Mass Signs Do to an Antithetic Pair}
\label{sec:motivation}

Before specifying the coupling we isolate the calculation that dictates it.
Consider one diffusion stage and condition on the state of both simulations
immediately before it. Let a particle of simulation $A$ sit at $a$ with mass
$\alpha$, and let the particle matched to it in simulation $B$ sit at $b$ with
mass $\beta$. After the stage their positions are
\[
 X_A=a+\sigma Z_A,\qquad X_B=b+\sigma Z_B,
\]
with $Z_A,Z_B\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$; the coupling is the choice of their joint
law. Abbreviate the two indicators by
\[
 \chi_A=\ind\{X_A\le x\},\qquad \chi_B=\ind\{X_B\le x\}.
\]
By \eqref{eq:representation} the pair contributes
$\tfrac12(\alpha\chi_A+\beta\chi_B)$ to the pair mean at the point $x$.
We construct the increment pairs independently across matched ranks. Under
this restriction, contributions of distinct matched pairs are uncorrelated,
and the conditional variance of the pair mean decomposes over pairs. For the
pair above, the contribution is
\begin{equation}
 \tfrac14\Bigl[\alpha^2\Var\chi_A+\beta^2\Var\chi_B
  +2\,\alpha\beta\,\mathrm{Cov}(\chi_A,\chi_B)\Bigr].
 \label{eq:pairvar}
\end{equation}
The first two terms depend only on the marginal laws of $X_A$ and $X_B$, which
every admissible coupling leaves alone. The coupling acts through the third
term, and through it alone.

Write
\[
 p=\Phi\!\Bigl(\frac{x-a}{\sigma}\Bigr),
 \qquad
 q=\Phi\!\Bigl(\frac{x-b}{\sigma}\Bigr)
\]
for the two marginal probabilities, $\Phi$ being the standard normal
distribution function. The covariance in \eqref{eq:pairvar} is
$\mathrm{Cov}(\chi_A,\chi_B)=\Pr\{X_A\le x,\,X_B\le x\}-pq$, and the joint
probability is constrained by
the classical Fréchet--Hoeffding bounds
\citep[Theorem~2.2.3 and Section~2.5]{nelsen2006},
\begin{equation}
 \max(p+q-1,\,0)\;\le\;\Pr\{X_A\le x,\ X_B\le x\}\;\le\;\min(p,q),
 \label{eq:frechet}
\end{equation}
which hold for every coupling of the two marginals. Both bounds are attained
here, and by the two coupling choices that are actually available. Taking
$Z_B=-Z_A$ makes $X_B$ a strictly decreasing function of $X_A$, so the pair is
countermonotone and the left bound in \eqref{eq:frechet} is an equality;
taking $Z_B=Z_A$ makes them comonotone and the right bound is an equality.
Writing
\begin{equation}
 c_-(x)=\max(p+q-1,0)-pq\;\le\;0,
 \qquad
 c_+(x)=\min(p,q)-pq\;\ge\;0,
 \label{eq:cpm}
\end{equation}
reflection therefore realizes $c_-$ and synchronization realizes $c_+$, and no
other coupling of the two increments can leave the covariance outside
$[c_-(x),c_+(x)]$.

Restoring the mass product explains which endpoint we need. To minimize the
pair's contribution to conditional variance, we make
$\alpha\beta\,\mathrm{Cov}$ as negative as possible. Reflection does this when
$\alpha\beta>0$, and synchronization does it when $\alpha\beta<0$. We record
this consequence of the classical bounds for the present reconstruction.

\begin{proposition}[pairwise extremality of the switching rule]
\label{prop:pairwise}
Fix a diffusion stage, a matching, and the state of both simulations before the
stage. For a matched pair with positions $a,b$ and nonzero masses $\alpha,\beta$,
consider all joint laws of $(Z_A,Z_B)$ with standard normal marginals. The
contribution \eqref{eq:pairvar} of that pair to $\Var\bigl(\bar U(x)\bigr)$ is
minimized, simultaneously for every $x\in\R$, by
\[
 Z_B=\varepsilon\,Z_A,\qquad \varepsilon=-\sgn(\alpha)\sgn(\beta),
\]
and the minimum equals
$\tfrac14[\alpha^2 p(1-p)+\beta^2 q(1-q)+2\alpha\beta\,c_{\mp}(x)]$ with
$c_-$ taken when $\alpha\beta>0$ and $c_+$ when $\alpha\beta<0$. Reflecting a
pair with $\alpha\beta<0$ attains the pointwise \emph{maximum} instead.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The first two terms of \eqref{eq:pairvar} are functions of the marginals alone.
By \eqref{eq:frechet} the covariance lies in $[c_-(x),c_+(x)]$ for every
coupling, and the two stated choices attain the endpoints because
countermonotone and comonotone pairs realize the Fréchet--Hoeffding bounds.
Multiplying by $2\alpha\beta$ reverses the ordering when $\alpha\beta<0$, which
gives the claim and its last sentence.
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:pairwise} explains how to choose the multiplier for a
fixed matched pair. It does
not settle the choice of matching, since a different matching changes which
pairs exist, and the bounds \eqref{eq:frechet} are then taken at different
$(a,b)$. Nor does it extend along the horizon: the state entering a later stage
depends on the coupling used at earlier stages, so a stage-by-stage optimum is
not a terminal optimum. This division is the organizing principle of the paper.
The multiplier is fixed by Proposition~\ref{prop:pairwise}; the choice between
matching by spatial rank and matching within sign classes is not settled by it,
and is decided numerically in Section~\ref{sec:numerics}.

\subsection{The Coupling and Two Alternatives}
\label{sec:rules}

Both simulations start from the same deterministic initial state and are
advanced in lockstep. At stage $k$ let
\[
  S_k=\bigl(X^A,m^A,v^A,X^B,m^B,v^B\bigr)
\]
denote the joint state after the sort and velocity update of
Algorithm~\ref{alg:single} and before the diffusion, and let $\Fil_k$ denote
the information generated by the complete joint history up to that point,
including all earlier random draws. A rule measurable with respect to $\Fil_k$
uses only what is known before the next draw is made.

\begin{definition}[admissible coupling]
\label{def:coupling}
An \emph{admissible coupling} selects, at each stage $k$, a bijection
$\pi_k:\{1,\dots,N\}\to\{1,\dots,N\}$ and multipliers
$\varepsilon_k\in\{-1,+1\}^N$, both $\Fil_k$-measurable, draws
$Z^{(k)}\sim\mathcal N(0,I_N)$ independent of $\Fil_k$ and of all earlier
draws, and sets
\begin{equation}
  X^A_i \leftarrow X^A_i+\sigma Z^{(k)}_i,
  \qquad
  X^B_j \leftarrow X^B_j+\sigma\,\varepsilon_{k,j}\,Z^{(k)}_{\pi_k(j)} .
  \label{eq:coupling}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

Three admissible couplings are compared throughout. All give simulation $A$ the
same increments and differ only in $(\pi_k,\varepsilon_k)$.

\begin{description}
\item[\refl{} (reflected pairing).] $\pi_k=\mathrm{id}$ and
  $\varepsilon_{k,j}=-1$: match at equal spatial rank and reflect every pair.
  This is the direct transcription of antithetic sampling and serves as the
  common baseline.
\item[\swi{} (sign-switched pairing).] $\pi_k=\mathrm{id}$ and
  \begin{equation}
    \varepsilon_{k,j}=-\,\sgn\!\bigl(m^A_j\bigr)\,\sgn\!\bigl(m^B_j\bigr),
    \label{eq:switch}
  \end{equation}
  the rule of Proposition~\ref{prop:pairwise}: match at equal spatial rank,
  reflect a pair whose masses agree in sign, synchronize a pair whose masses
  differ.
\item[\wit{} (within-sign pairing).] $\pi_k$ sends the $r$th particle of sign
  $s$ in $B$ to the $r$th particle of sign $s$ in $A$, for $s\in\{+,-\}$, and
  $\varepsilon_{k,j}=-1$: restrict the matching so that no pair has unlike
  masses, then reflect every pair.
\end{description}

By Proposition~\ref{prop:pairwise}, \refl{} and \swi{} agree on every pair with
like masses and disagree on every pair with unlike masses, where \refl{} makes
precisely the worst available choice. \wit{} avoids unlike pairs altogether, at
the price of matching particles that may be far apart; Figure~\ref{fig:construction}
shows both matchings on one state. Algorithm~\ref{alg:pair} gives the coupled
advance.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Coupled advance of two simulations.}
\label{alg:pair}
\SetAlgoLined
\KwIn{common initial state $(X^0,m^0)$ sorted by position, $u_-$, steps $K$,
      step $h$, viscosity $\nu$, flux derivative $f'$, policy
      $\in\{\refl,\swi,\wit\}$}
$(X^A,m^A)\leftarrow(X^0,m^0)$; \quad $(X^B,m^B)\leftarrow(X^0,m^0)$;
  \quad $\sigma\leftarrow\sqrt{2\nu h}$\;
$v^A_i\leftarrow f'\!\bigl(u_-+\sum_{j\le i}m^A_j\bigr)$; \quad $v^B\leftarrow v^A$\;
\For{$k=0,\dots,K-1$}{
  \ForEach{simulation $Q\in\{A,B\}$}{
    $X^Q_i\leftarrow X^Q_i+v^Q_ih$; \quad stable-sort $(X^Q,m^Q)$ by $X^Q$; \quad
    $v^Q_i\leftarrow f'\!\bigl(u_-+\sum_{j\le i}m^Q_j\bigr)$\;
  }
  \uIf{policy $=\refl$}{$\pi\leftarrow\mathrm{id}$; \quad $\varepsilon_j\leftarrow-1$\;}
  \uElseIf{policy $=\swi$}{$\pi\leftarrow\mathrm{id}$; \quad
    $\varepsilon_j\leftarrow-\sgn(m^A_j)\sgn(m^B_j)$\;}
  \Else(\tcp*[h]{$\wit$}){\ForEach{$s\in\{+1,-1\}$}{
      $I_s\leftarrow\{i:\sgn(m^A_i)=s\}$, $J_s\leftarrow\{j:\sgn(m^B_j)=s\}$,
        both increasing; \quad assert $|I_s|=|J_s|$\;
      $\pi(J_s[r])\leftarrow I_s[r]$ for $r=1,\dots,|I_s|$; \quad
        $\varepsilon\leftarrow-1$ on those $j$\;
      }}
  draw $Z\sim\mathcal N(0,I_N)$ independent of the joint history\;
  $X^A_i\leftarrow X^A_i+\sigma Z_i$; \quad
  $X^B_j\leftarrow X^B_j+\sigma\varepsilon_jZ_{\pi(j)}$\;
}
\KwOut{$(X^A,m^A)$, $(X^B,m^B)$, and the pair mean \eqref{eq:est-pair}}
\end{algorithm}

Four conditions in Definition~\ref{def:coupling} are needed for the law
preservation of Section~\ref{sec:law}, and each can be violated by an
implementation. The vector $Z^{(k)}$ must be freshly drawn and independent of
the whole joint history; reusing one coordinate for two particles of the same
simulation preserves each particle's marginal but changes the joint transition.
The map $\pi_k$ must be a bijection, so that no coordinate of $Z^{(k)}$ is
consumed twice. The multipliers must have unit modulus, since any other value
rescales the increment. Zero masses must be excluded or given a convention,
because $\sgn(0)=0$ in \eqref{eq:switch} would suppress a particle's diffusion
altogether; our initialization gives $|m_i|=\mathrm{TV}(u_0)/N>0$ and the
implementation rejects a zero mass. Finally, \wit{} additionally requires equal
sign-class counts in the two simulations, which the common deterministic
initialization and the fixed masses guarantee.

\subsection{Each Simulation Keeps its Law}
\label{sec:law}

The construction changes how the two simulations are related while preserving
the law of each one. We can therefore retain results about a single simulation
and its expectation; the variance of the coupled average is the quantity that
requires a new comparison.

\begin{proposition}[law preservation]
\label{prop:law}
Let two simulations be advanced by Algorithm~\ref{alg:single} from the same
initial state under any admissible coupling. Then the law of the complete
state trajectory $\bigl(X^A_k,m^A_k,v^A_k\bigr)_{k=0}^{K}$ of simulation $A$,
and likewise that of simulation $B$, is exactly the law of an uncoupled single
simulation. Only the joint law of the two is changed.
\end{proposition}

The proof, in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}, is an induction over stages: a
predictable permutation of an i.i.d.\ Gaussian vector is i.i.d.\ Gaussian, and
predictable $\pm1$ multipliers preserve $\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$ coordinatewise,
so each simulation's one-step transition kernel is unchanged given the past.
The same invariance of the Gaussian law under adapted orthogonal maps is used
by Nüsken and Pavliotis~\citep[Lemma~74, Appendix A]{nusken2018}.

\begin{remark}[what the proposition does and does not require]
\label{rem:hyp}
The argument uses the conditional Gaussian transition and the fact that all
remaining operations of Algorithm~\ref{alg:single} are deterministic functions
of a single simulation's own state. It does not require the particle signs to
be constant in time, nor the output functional to be linear in the particle
measure. The present solver does keep signs fixed, but a scheme that updated
masses would still preserve each simulation's law provided the updates are
functions of that simulation's own state and the multipliers remain predictable
and of unit modulus.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}[shared bias]
\label{cor:bias}
Under any admissible coupling, $\E\bar U=\E U^{\mathrm{sing}}$ pointwise
whenever these expectations exist. Hence the squared bias $b^2$ in
\eqref{eq:decomposition} takes the same value for \refl{}, \swi{} and \wit{},
and equals that of the uncoupled solver.
\end{corollary}

The corollary follows from Proposition~\ref{prop:law} and linearity of the
expectation across the two simulations, and it holds for the average of any
integrable per-simulation functional. It does not hold for a nonlinear function
of the pair mean, a distinction that becomes quantitative in
Section~\ref{sec:observables}. Corollary~\ref{cor:bias} also fixes the terms of
every comparison below: since all three pairings share $b^2$, they can be
compared through $V$ alone, and the planning rule of Section~\ref{sec:work} may
use one estimate of $b^2$ for all of them. What the corollary does not do is
license any statement about the continuum: preserving a discrete law preserves
whatever error that discrete law already has.

\subsection{An Exact Identity for One Diffusion Stage}
\label{sec:identity}

Proposition~\ref{prop:pairwise} compares couplings pointwise in $x$. Integrating
that comparison over the line turns it into a single number, and the result is
an exact expression for the variance that the switching rule removes at one
stage. The following elementary identity supplies the integration.

\begin{lemma}[kernel identity]
\label{lem:wholeline}
Fix $c\in\R$ and set $h_a(x)=\ind\{x\ge a\}-\ind\{x\ge c\}$, a function
supported on the interval between $a$ and $c$. For all $a,b\in\R$,
\begin{equation}
  \int_{\R}h_a(x)\,h_b(x)\,\mathrm dx
  \;=\;\tfrac12\bigl(|a-c|+|b-c|-|a-b|\bigr).
  \label{eq:kernel}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

The verification is a case check on the order of $a$, $b$ and $c$ and is given
in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}. Writing the deviation of a reconstructed field
from its far-field values in terms of these kernels lets the covariance of two
reconstructions be expressed through the distances between their particles.

\begin{proposition}[final-stage identity]
\label{prop:finalstage}
Let $g_s(d)=\E\,|d+sZ|$ with $Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$. Condition on the paired
state immediately before the last diffusion stage, let $a_i$ and $b_i$ be the
positions of the particles matched at rank $i$, let $d_i=a_i-b_i$, and let
$m^A_i,m^B_i$ be their masses. Write
\[
 V_{\bullet}=\int_{\R}\Var\bigl(\bar U(x)\mid\Fil_{K-1}\bigr)\,\mathrm dx
\]
for the conditional whole-line integrated variance of the pair mean under
coupling $\bullet$. Then
\begin{equation}
  V_{\refl}-V_{\swi}
  \;=\;\frac14\!\!\sum_{i:\,m^A_i m^B_i<0}\!\!
    \bigl|m^A_i m^B_i\bigr|\,
    \Bigl[g_{2\sigma}(d_i)-|d_i|\Bigr]\;\ge\;0 .
  \label{eq:finalstage}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

Three features of \eqref{eq:finalstage} are worth reading off. Only pairs of
unlike mass contribute, as Proposition~\ref{prop:pairwise} requires, since the
two couplings agree elsewhere. Each contributing pair enters in proportion to
the product of its mass magnitudes. And its contribution is governed by
$g_{2\sigma}(d)-|d|\ge0$, an increment that is largest when the matched
separation $|d|$ is small compared with the diffusion scale $\sigma$ and decays
as $|d|/\sigma$ grows; the non-negativity is Jensen's inequality. Rank matching
keeps $|d|$ small, which is one reason to prefer it, though not a proof that it
wins overall.

\begin{remark}[hypotheses and scope]
\label{rem:finalstage-scope}
The identity requires no condition on the total mass \eqref{eq:total-mass}: the
constant by which a reconstruction differs from its kernel representation is
deterministic and cancels in the covariance, as the proof makes explicit. When
$\sum_i m_i=0$ the reference point $c$ in \eqref{eq:kernel} also drops out and
one recovers the familiar product identity
$\int_\R D_AD_B=-\tfrac12\sum_{i,j}m^A_im^B_j|X^A_i-X^B_j|$ for the
centered reconstructions $D_\bullet$. Equation~\eqref{eq:finalstage} is a
statement about one stage, conditional on the state entering it; it does not
assert that switching helps whenever signs disagree at an earlier stage,
because the distribution of later states is itself coupling-dependent. It is a
whole-line statement, and is never substituted for the windowed norm of
Remark~\ref{rem:window}.
\end{remark}

Two independent numerical checks of \eqref{eq:finalstage} are reported in
Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}: a direct paired Monte Carlo over the final stage at
six pre-final states of the two-pulse problem, and a quadrature of the joint
indicator probabilities at a configuration with unequal mass magnitudes.
Applied to the archived $64$-seed runs, the identity accounts for
$3.75\%$, $3.71\%$ and $1.95\%$ of the total measured reduction
$V_{\refl}-V_{\swi}$ at three configurations, against measured single-stage
shares of $4.23\%$, $5.23\%$ and $2.91\%$. The exact result therefore explains
a mechanism and a few percent of the effect; the remainder accumulates over the
history and is measured, not proved.

\subsection{When the Two Couplings Coincide}
\label{sec:equality}

\begin{lemma}[conditional identity]
\label{lem:conditional}
Start \refl{} and \swi{} from the same state and drive them with the same
sequence of draws. Their complete states coincide at every stage up to and
including the first stage at which some matched pair has unlike masses. If no
such stage occurs before $K$, the two runs are identical.
\end{lemma}

The proof is immediate from \eqref{eq:switch}: while every matched pair has
like masses, $\varepsilon_{k,j}=-1$, which is \refl{}.

\begin{corollary}[one-sign case]
\label{cor:onesign}
If all gradient masses share one sign, the hypothesis of
Lemma~\ref{lem:conditional} holds for every history, and \swi{} and \refl{}
are the same algorithm.
\end{corollary}

Corollary~\ref{cor:onesign} is a genuine limitation rather than a curiosity:
for monotone initial data the construction does exactly nothing, and no
experiment can show otherwise. We verified it on the complete state, positions,
masses and carried velocities, for $200$ seeds under both heat and Burgers
dynamics, with maximum absolute difference exactly zero in every seed.

It is tempting to expect the same conclusion when the positive and negative
regions merely start far apart, since the rank order then groups particles by
sign. That expectation is false, and the reason is elementary: Gaussian support
is unbounded, so for two particles separated by $d$ the probability that
exactly one of the two simulations reverses their order is
$\Pr\{|Z_1-Z_2|>d/\sigma\}>0$ at every finite $d$. Once an order reverses, a
matched pair of unlike mass appears and the two couplings separate. On the
spatially separated configuration of Section~\ref{sec:structure} at $N=2048$
with $200$ fresh seeds, the two couplings diverge in $58/200=0.290$
$[0.228,0.358]$ of seeds under heat dynamics and $59/200=0.295$ $[0.233,0.363]$
under Burgers, with median first divergence at step $187$ of $200$.

\subsection{Two Routes That Are Closed}
\label{sec:closed}

The pairwise result of Section~\ref{sec:motivation} is exact but local, and the
identity of Section~\ref{sec:identity} is exact but confined to one stage. It
is natural to ask what blocks an unconditional terminal statement. Two obvious
routes are closed, and saying so is more useful than leaving them open.

\begin{remark}[coordinatewise monotonicity fails]
\label{rem:mono}
Antithetic sampling is guaranteed to help when the response is monotone in each
input, so one might hope to establish that the terminal continuation value is
monotone in each particle position, with an orientation set by the mass sign.
It is not. In a two-particle, transport-dominated configuration
($m=\pm\tfrac12$, $h=0.005$, $\nu=10^{-10}$, hence $\sigma=10^{-6}$ against a
transport displacement of $10^{-2}$), the conditional expectation
$\E[U(x,T)\mid\text{state}]$ at a probe point \emph{increases} by $0.5$ when a
positive-mass particle is displaced to the right, the opposite of the required
orientation. The configuration was traced against the solver over $512$ seeds,
every seed reproducing the deterministic outcome, and the probability of an
exceptional draw is bounded analytically by $\exp(-5003)$.
Appendix~\ref{app:counterexample} gives the trace.
\end{remark}

The counterexample rules out the unrestricted argument. It does not show that
\swi{} is ever worse than \refl{}, and the configuration is far outside the
diffusion-dominated regime: its ratio of mass-dependent transport to diffusion
scale is $R=|m|h/\sigma=2500$. Whether a terminal comparison can be proved under
a small-$R$ assumption remains open.

\begin{remark}[sorted matching is optimal for absolute distance]
\label{rem:sorted}
The second route would compare the two matchings geometrically. For two equally
sized finite sets of reals listed in increasing order, the identity bijection
minimizes $\sum_i|a_i-b_{\pi(i)}|$ over all bijections $\pi$; this is the
monotone rearrangement, which is the optimal transport map on the line for any
convex cost \citep{villani2003}. Rank matching \emph{is} that bijection, and
within-sign matching is a bijection constrained to respect the sign classes, so
\begin{equation}
  \sum_i\bigl|a_i-b_i\bigr|\;\le\;\sum_i\bigl|a_i-b_{\pi_{\wit}(i)}\bigr|
  \label{eq:sortedopt}
\end{equation}
with probability one.
\end{remark}

Inequality \eqref{eq:sortedopt} tells us that rank matching always produces the
closer partners, and by Proposition~\ref{prop:finalstage} close partners are
what the final-stage gain rewards. It does not tell us that rank matching wins,
because the two rules differ in which pairs exist and therefore in which mass
products multiply those distances. In particular, any measured ratio of
matched separations is bounded above by $1$ by construction, so such a ratio
reports the size of the geometric penalty imposed by the sign-class constraint
and cannot by itself identify the cause of a variance difference. We report it
in Section~\ref{sec:profiles} on that understanding.

\section{Numerical Results}
\label{sec:numerics}

Section~\ref{sec:coupling} fixes the multiplier and leaves the matching open.
The experiments below therefore lead with the comparison between \swi{} and
\wit{}, the two sign-aware rules, and use \refl{} as the common baseline
against which both are measured. Six tests are reported: the benchmark
comparison of the three pairings; a comparison with two standard
variance-reduction techniques applied to the same solver; a variation of the
initial profile, the observation time and the flux; execution time at
prescribed accuracy with plans frozen before evaluation; nonlinear functionals
and the bias-dominated regime; and a set of signed-structure controls that
locate the configurations in which the construction does nothing.

\subsection{Test Configuration and Statistical Conventions}
\label{sec:setup}

Unless stated otherwise the test problem is the asymmetric signed two-pulse
initial condition
\begin{equation}
  u_0(x)=0.8\,e^{-(x+0.7)^2/(2\cdot 0.5^2)}-0.5\,e^{-(x-0.4)^2/(2\cdot 0.3^2)},
  \label{eq:twopulse}
\end{equation}
with $f(u)=u^2/2$, $\nu=0.1$, $T=1$ and $h=0.005$, hence $K=200$ steps,
reconstructed on $\Omega=[-5,5]$ at $L=400$ points. Its derivative has three
resolved sign regions, so both sign classes are populated and the classes
interleave once; unlike-sign matched pairs therefore occur, which is the
situation the coupling addresses. Reference solutions are Fourier
pseudospectral on $[-16,16]$ with $M=2048$ modes, $\tfrac23$ dealiasing and an
integrating-factor RK4 step of $10^{-4}$. For Burgers this agrees with a
stabilized whole-line Cole--Hopf evaluation to $5.6\times10^{-14}$ in maximum
norm, and for the cubic flux it self-converges to $1.6\times10^{-13}$; both are
negligible beside the field errors reported below, which are of order
$10^{-6}$. Appendix~\ref{app:setup} gives the initializations and reference
calculations.

Every experiment uses recorded deterministic seed keys, and independent
replicates use distinct streams. Whether two comparison arms share a stream is
recorded with each experiment, because it determines the resampling scheme:
bootstrap intervals resample complete realizations \citep{efron1981}, and arms
that share a stream are resampled together so that their dependence is
preserved. The main field, control and nonlinear-error tables use $6000$
percentile-bootstrap resamples; the profile/time and structure tables retain
their archived $3000$ and $2000$. Variance ratios of scalar station observables
use $F(n-1,n-1)$ intervals for independent arms. Held-out errors use $t$
intervals on $32$ independent block errors, with a Bonferroni correction across
the arms of each experiment. Divergence frequencies use exact binomial
intervals. Appendix~\ref{app:uncertainty} reports a coverage calibration of the
two scalar interval procedures and states its limits.

Throughout, a ratio below $1$ favors the numerator, and an interval containing
$1$ means the comparison is unresolved at the sample size used. Such an
interval records the absence of evidence, not evidence of absence.

is a statement about these two controls at these configurations, not a claim
that no control can help. Third, conditional expectation is complementary
rather than competing: applied on top of the switched coupling it reduces
relative variance from $0.1649$ to $0.0804$ and gives the smallest
variance-times-cost in the table, $0.151$. Its benefit depends on how many
conditional evaluations the output requires; raising that number from $201$ to
$1601$ moves the variance-cost ratio of adding the conditional stage from
$0.614$ to $1.485$. We therefore report the coupling and the conditional stage
separately, so that neither inflates the other.

A fourth alternative, a randomized quasi-Monte Carlo adaptation of the
diffusion construction of Lécot~\citep{lecot_rims2001}, is an arm of
Test~4 in Section~\ref{sec:work}, where it attains the common target with
$46$ trajectories against four.

extrapolate to general $f$.

\subsection{Test 4: Execution Time at a Prescribed Accuracy}
\label{sec:work}

Variance ratios at fixed settings are not work. The fourth test measures
execution time at a specified expected error, with the estimator plan fixed
before the evaluation data are seen. For each arm a preliminary run supplied
estimates of the squared bias, the integrated variance, and the cost per
replicate; the replicate count was then set to the smallest positive integer
$B$ with
\begin{equation}
 b^2_{\mathrm{hi}}+\frac{V_{\mathrm{hi}}}{B}\;\le\;\frac{\mathrm{tol}}{\kappa},
 \qquad \kappa=1.3,
 \label{eq:plan}
\end{equation}
using upper interval endpoints and the headroom factor $\kappa$; and the frozen
plan was executed in $32$ independent blocks on an unused seed stream, a block
being one complete $B$-replicate estimate. By Corollary~\ref{cor:bias} the
three pairings and the unpaired ensemble share a bias, so one pilot estimate of
$b^2$, taken from the switched arm, serves all of them; the quasi-Monte Carlo
arm does not preserve the baseline law and uses its own. No estimate is
truncated at zero: the squared bias is estimated as
$\|\hat{\bar U}-u_{\mathrm{ref}}\|^2_\Omega-V/B_{\mathrm{pilot}}$, which is
unbiased and may take small negative values, and it is the upper endpoint
$b^2_{\mathrm{hi}}$ that enters \eqref{eq:plan}.

The two experiments differ in target, particle count and comparators, and we
report them separately rather than pooling them. Experiment~A was designed
before within-sign pairing entered the study and does not contain it; it
compares the switched coupling with an unpaired ensemble, with reflected
pairing, and with the quasi-Monte Carlo adaptation. Experiment~B compares the
three pairings.

\subsubsection{Experiment A: Unpaired and Quasi-Monte Carlo Comparators}
\label{sec:heldoutA}

Experiment~A uses $N=8192$ and targets an expected integrated squared error of
$7\times10^{-6}$, with pilot key $1601$ and evaluation key $1702$. The target
was chosen from the pilot and is therefore pilot-informed rather than
independent of the data.

The quasi-Monte Carlo arm adapts Lécot's rank-and-increment map
\citep[Eq. (45)]{lecot_rims2001} to our transport update. One scrambled Sobol'
sequence is generated per replicate, and successive time steps consume
successive blocks of $N$ points; the first coordinate assigns a rank by
$\lfloor Np_1\rfloor$ and the second supplies the increment
$\sigma\Phi^{-1}(p_2)$. This is an adaptation and not a reproduction of the
published method, which uses a different transport rule and a Faure sequence;
its interacting law differs from the baseline, so its bias is estimated
separately and its total error is what the comparison uses.
Appendix~\ref{app:comparators} records the engine settings, the scrambling, and
the endpoint handling.

\subsection{Test 5: Observables and the Bias-Dominated Regime}
\label{sec:observables}

\subsubsection{Dependence on Position}

At $N=8192$ with $48$ replicates on a separate seed key, the variance ratio of
\swi{} to \refl{} at four reconstruction points is $0.636$ $[0.357,1.135]$ at
the left flank $x=-2.0$, $0.143$ $[0.080,0.255]$ at the left pulse $x=-0.7$,
$0.093$ $[0.052,0.166]$ at the steepest core $x=+0.1$, and $0.735$
$[0.412,1.311]$ at the right flank $x=+1.5$, with $F(47,47)$ intervals. The two
flank comparisons are unresolved. The reduction is a property of the region
where the field varies, which is also where the particles are.

\subsubsection{Nonlinear Functionals}

For a nonlinear functional $Q$ the two natural estimators are not the same
object. Applying $Q$ to the pair mean gives $Q(\bar U)$, whereas averaging the
functional over the two simulations gives
$\tfrac12\bigl(Q(U^{(A)})+Q(U^{(B)})\bigr)$. By Proposition~\ref{prop:law} the
second has exactly the expectation of $Q$ evaluated on a single uncoupled
simulation, so its bias is the single-simulation bias whatever the coupling;
the first enjoys no such guarantee, because Corollary~\ref{cor:bias} does not
survive a nonlinear map. A variance ratio is therefore not an accuracy ratio,
and we report the empirical mean squared error against the reference,
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\mathrm{MSE}}
  =\frac1n\sum_{r=1}^{n}\bigl(Q_r-Q_{\mathrm{ref}}\bigr)^2
  =\widehat{\mathrm{bias}}^{\,2}+\frac{n-1}{n}\,s^2 ,
  \label{eq:empmse}
\end{equation}
rather than the plug-in $\widehat{\mathrm{bias}}^{\,2}+s^2$, which exceeds
\eqref{eq:empmse} by $s^2/n$, here $0.7\%$ to $2.0\%$. Both functionals are
rule-dependent and we state the rules: $\max|\partial_x u|$ uses central
differences of the reconstruction on the output grid, and the level crossing is
the first downward crossing of $u=0.25$ to the right of the global maximum, by
linear interpolation, with replicates lacking such a crossing recorded as
censored. None occurred in any cell.

\subsubsection{The Bias-Dominated Regime}
\label{sec:biasregime}

The same arithmetic governs the field itself. Since the couplings share $b^2$
by Corollary~\ref{cor:bias}, \eqref{eq:decomposition} gives
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\mathcal E_{\swi}(B)}{\mathcal E_{\refl}(B)}
  =\frac{b^2+V_{\swi}/B}{b^2+V_{\refl}/B}\;\longrightarrow\;1
  \qquad\text{as } B\to\infty ,
  \label{eq:crossover}
\end{equation}
so at fixed $N$ and $h$ any coupling advantage is a small-$B$ phenomenon. The
scale on which it fades is $B^{\star}=V/b^2$, at which the sampling and
squared-bias contributions are equal; for the switched arm this is $10.3$ at
$N=6400,\,h=0.0025$, $4.5$ at $N=6400,\,h=0.005$, and $35.3$ at
$N=1600,\,h=0.0025$.

Comparing at equal $B$ is not the same as comparing at equal expense, since the
two arms have different per-replicate costs $C_{\swi}$ and $C_{\refl}$. Setting
$B_\bullet=W/C_\bullet$ for a common work budget $W$ gives the quantity plotted
in Figure~\ref{fig:bias},
\begin{equation}
 \frac{b^2+V_{\swi}C_{\swi}/W}{b^2+V_{\refl}C_{\refl}/W},
 \label{eq:equalwork}
\end{equation}
whose limit as $W\to\infty$ is again $1$. The crossover scale becomes
$W=C_{\swi}V_{\swi}/b^2$, marked by the circles in the figure. It is a
crossover and not a cutoff: there the ratio is still $0.18$ to $0.26$, and it
approaches $1$ only some two decades of work later.

The practical reading is the one a user can act on. Coupling buys accuracy
while sampling error still contributes materially to the requested quantity,
which is the regime in which one is paying for solver trajectories. Once
discretization error dominates, the remedy is to refine $N$ or $h$, and no
coupling will substitute for that.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

Averaging repeated simulations is a simple way to reduce sampling error for
a signed gradient-particle solver. The mass signs help us choose how to couple
those simulations. At a fixed pre-stage state, reflection maximizes an
opposite-sign pair's contribution to conditional variance, while synchronous
increments minimize it; for a same-sign pair, reflection minimizes it. These
pairwise statements hold simultaneously at every reconstruction point. The
resulting switch leaves each simulation's discrete law intact, so the averaged
field retains the expectation and bias of the underlying solver. The coupling
changes its sampling variance, and the reduction at a single diffusion stage
has a closed form in terms of mass products and matched separations.

The choice of matching requires a separate comparison. Restricting pairs to
sign classes allows reflection to be used throughout, and the pairwise
argument alone cannot distinguish that construction from the spatially matched
switch. In the principal Burgers experiment, within-sign matching captures
most of the reduction relative to reflected rank pairing. The switched rule
reduces the integrated variance further, to $0.739$ $[0.682,0.801]$ of the
within-sign value at $0.94$ times its measured cost; its variance relative to
reflected pairing is $0.127$ $[0.107,0.151]$. The profile and time comparisons
support an advantage over within-sign pairing for three profiles at the two
later observation times, and the flux comparison shows that the benefit also
occurs with cubic transport. In held-out experiment~B, the switched rule reaches
the prescribed expected-error target with four trajectories, compared with six
for within-sign pairing and thirty-six for reflected pairing. Experiment~A
provides a separate comparison with an unpaired ensemble and the RQMC
adaptation at a different target and particle count.

The boundaries help identify where the construction is useful. When all
gradient masses share one sign, switching gives exactly the same paths as
reflected pairing, although pairing itself can still help relative to
independent runs. No advantage over reflection is resolved in the tested
separated-sign configuration, on the flat flanks of a profile, or for the
bias-dominated peak-gradient functional. The total-error benefit also fades
as discretization bias dominates, on a scale set by $B^\star=V/b^2$.
Conditional expectation at the final diffusion stage offers a complementary
reduction: in the tested configurations it improves the switched estimator's
variance-times-cost further.

Three questions seem worth pursuing. Whether a terminal variance comparison
holds under explicit resolution assumptions is open; Remark~\ref{rem:mono}
rules out the unrestricted coordinatewise argument, while conditions such as
$|m|h/\sigma\ll1$ remain to be investigated. Whether the construction extends to solvers
that sample a velocity label, where the coupling would have to act on the label
draw as well as on the diffusion, is a design question rather than a corollary,
and the relaxation gradient-particle schemes of Bertaglia, Pareschi and
Caflisch~\citep{bertaglia2024} are the natural setting in which to ask it.
Finally, the cost of the construction in a parallel implementation, where the
two simulations must exchange matched masses at every stage, requires its own
measurement.

\section{Proofs}
\label{app:proofs}

\subsection{Proposition~\ref{prop:law}}

Let $\Xi^A_k=(X^A_k,m^A_k,v^A_k)$ be the complete state entering
stage $k$, with $k=0,\ldots,K-1$. Let $\Fil_k$ contain the joint history
through the sort and velocity update of that stage, as in
Definition~\ref{def:coupling}. The deterministic part of
Algorithm~\ref{alg:single} acts on $\Xi^A_k$ alone. We compare the conditional
increment laws before constructing $\Xi^A_{k+1}$.

Replica $A$ receives $\sigma Z^{(k)}$, with
$Z^{(k)}\sim\mathcal N(0,I_N)$ independent of $\Fil_k$. Replica $B$ receives
\[
 \Delta X^B_j=\sigma\varepsilon_{k,j}Z^{(k)}_{\pi_k(j)}.
\]
Given $\Fil_k$, the matching and sign vector are fixed. A permutation of
independent standard Gaussian coordinates preserves their joint law, and
multiplication by fixed signs preserves both their distributions and
independence. Therefore
\[
 \mathcal L(\Delta X^A\mid\Fil_k)
 =\mathcal L(\Delta X^B\mid\Fil_k)
 =\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2 I_N).
\]
Each replica has the same conditional transition as the uncoupled solver.
Induction over the stages proves equality of its complete trajectory law
with the baseline law. The joint law of the two replicas changes because
their increments are dependent. \qed

Independence of the fresh vector and predictability of the rule justify
conditioning before the draw. Bijectivity ensures independence within each
replica, and unit-modulus multipliers preserve the variance. The particular
rule \eqref{eq:switch} also needs nonzero masses. Retaining each particle's
carried velocity in the state makes the deterministic update well defined.

\subsection{Lemma~\ref{lem:wholeline}}

Both $h_a$ and $h_b$ vanish outside the intervals with endpoints $a,c$ and
$b,c$ respectively, so the product is integrable. If $a$ and $b$ lie on the
same side of $c$, say $c\le a\le b$, then $h_a=h_b=-1$ on $[c,a)$, $h_b=-1$ and
$h_a=0$ on $[a,b)$, and both vanish elsewhere, so
$\int h_ah_b=|a-c|$, which equals $\tfrac12(|a-c|+|b-c|-|a-b|)$ because
$|b-c|=|a-c|+|b-a|$. The case $c\ge a\ge b$ is identical. If $a$ and $b$ lie on
opposite sides of $c$ the supports meet only at $c$ and the integral vanishes,
while $|a-b|=|a-c|+|b-c|$ makes the right-hand side vanish as well. \qed

\subsection{Proposition~\ref{prop:finalstage}}

Condition on the pre-final state and fix any reference point $c\in\R$. With
$h_a$ as in Lemma~\ref{lem:wholeline}, the terminal reconstruction of
simulation $A$ satisfies
\[
 U^{(A)}(x)=u_-+\sum_i m^A_i\,h_{X^A_i}(x)
  +\Bigl(\sum_i m^A_i\Bigr)\ind\{x\ge c\},
\]
and likewise for $B$. The last term is deterministic, so it does not affect any
variance or covariance; no condition on the total mass \eqref{eq:total-mass} is
therefore needed. Setting $G_A=\sum_i m^A_i h_{X^A_i}$ and
$G_B=\sum_j m^B_j h_{X^B_j}$, we may write
$\bar U(x)=D(x)+\tfrac12(G_A(x)+G_B(x))$, where $D(x)$ is deterministic under
this conditioning. Consequently,
\[
  V_\bullet=\int_\R\Var\bar U
  =\tfrac14\Bigl(\int\Var G_A+\int\Var G_B+2\int\mathrm{Cov}(G_A,G_B)\Bigr).
\]
By Proposition~\ref{prop:law} the first two integrals are the same for every
admissible coupling, so only the covariance has to be evaluated.

Expanding and applying \eqref{eq:kernel} to the coupled pair gives
\[
 \int_\R\E\bigl[G_AG_B\bigr]
 =\tfrac12\sum_{i,j}m^A_im^B_j
   \Bigl(\E\bigl|X^A_i-c\bigr|+\E\bigl|X^B_j-c\bigr|
         -\E\bigl|X^A_i-X^B_j\bigr|\Bigr).
\]
For $\int\E G_A\,\E G_B$, introduce an independent copy $X'^B_j$ of $X^B_j$;
since $\E G_B=\E G_{B'}$ pointwise, the same expansion applies with
$X^B_j$ replaced by $X'^B_j$, and the first two groups of terms are unchanged
because they involve only the marginals. Subtracting,
\[
  \int_\R\mathrm{Cov}(G_A,G_B)
  =-\tfrac12\sum_{i,j}m^A_im^B_j
     \Bigl[\E\bigl|X^A_i-X^B_j\bigr|-\E\bigl|X^A_i-X'^B_j\bigr|\Bigr],
\]
and $X^A_i-X'^B_j=(a_i-b_j)+\sigma\sqrt2\,\mathcal N(0,1)$ in law, so the
second expectation equals $g_{\sigma\sqrt2}(a_i-b_j)$.

For $i\ne j$ the increments applied to $X^A_i$ and $X^B_j$ are independent
under \refl{} and \swi{}, so
$X^A_i-X^B_j=(a_i-b_j)+\sigma\sqrt2\,\mathcal N(0,1)$ in law as well and the
bracket vanishes; only the diagonal survives. There, under \refl{},
$X^A_i-X^B_i=d_i+2\sigma Z_i$ and the first expectation is $g_{2\sigma}(d_i)$.
Under \swi{} the same holds when $m^A_im^B_i>0$, whereas when
$m^A_im^B_i<0$ the two increments cancel exactly and the first expectation is
$|d_i|$. Subtracting the two couplings,
\[
 \begin{aligned}
  \int\mathrm{Cov}_{\refl}-\int\mathrm{Cov}_{\swi}
  &=-\tfrac12\!\!\sum_{m^A_im^B_i<0}\!\! m^A_im^B_i
      \bigl[g_{2\sigma}(d_i)-|d_i|\bigr]\\
  &=\tfrac12\!\!\sum_{m^A_im^B_i<0}\!\!\bigl|m^A_im^B_i\bigr|
      \bigl[g_{2\sigma}(d_i)-|d_i|\bigr],
 \end{aligned}
\]
and multiplying by the factor $\tfrac12$ from the decomposition of $V_\bullet$
gives \eqref{eq:finalstage}. Non-negativity follows from Jensen's inequality,
\[
 g_s(d)=\E|d+sZ|\ \ge\ \bigl|\E(d+sZ)\bigr|=|d|. \qquad\qed
\]

\begin{remark}[recovering the product form]
When $\sum_i m^A_i=\sum_j m^B_j=0$ the terms involving the reference point $c$
cancel identically in the expansion of $\int\E[G_AG_B]$, and one obtains the
product identity
$\int_\R D_AD_B=-\tfrac12\sum_{i,j}m^A_im^B_j\bigl|X^A_i-X^B_j\bigr|$
quoted in Remark~\ref{rem:finalstage-scope}, with
$D_\bullet$ the reconstruction measured from its left far-field value.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Numerical Checks of the Final-Stage Identity}
\label{app:finalcheck}

The identity was checked by two routes that do not share the algebra of the
proof. The first is a direct paired Monte Carlo over the final stage at six
pre-final states drawn from the two-pulse problem, in which the conditional
integrated variance of the pair mean is estimated by quadrature on a fine grid
using the same draws for both couplings. All six states agreed with
\eqref{eq:finalstage} within a nominal $95\%$ interval built from $12\,000$
draws in $40$ batches, and refining the quadrature window by a factor of $2.7$
moved the estimate by $0.02\%$. The second evaluates the joint indicator
probabilities directly: for a matched pair at $a,b$ with
$p=\Phi((x-a)/\sigma)$ and $q=\Phi((x-b)/\sigma)$, reflection gives
$\Pr\{X_A\le x,X_B\le x\}=\max(p+q-1,0)$ and synchronization gives
$\min(p,q)$, so that
\[
 V_{\refl}-V_{\swi}
 =\tfrac12\int_\R\sum_{i:\,m^A_im^B_i<0} m^A_im^B_i
   \bigl[\max(p_i+q_i-1,0)-\min(p_i,q_i)\bigr]\,\mathrm dx
\]
because like-sign pairs use reflection under both rules and contribute zero
to the difference. Adaptive quadrature of this expression at a
configuration with four particles of unequal mass magnitudes agrees with
\eqref{eq:finalstage} to ten decimal places. The two expressions for the joint
probabilities are the Fréchet--Hoeffding bounds of \eqref{eq:frechet}. This
numerical check verifies their use in the integrated formula at that
configuration; the argument in Section~\ref{sec:motivation} establishes the
pairwise result for all admissible bivariate couplings.

\section{A Two-Particle Monotonicity Counterexample}
\label{app:counterexample}
Take $u_-=0$, $m=(1/2,-1/2)$, $h=0.005$, $\nu=10^{-10}$, and two steps.
The initial positions are $(p,0)$, with $p_L=-0.00275$ or
$p_R=-0.00225$. Both initial carried velocities are $(1/2,0)$.
Without diffusion, the first transport puts the positive particle at
$-0.00025$ in state $L$ and $+0.00025$ in state $R$. Re-sorting therefore
leaves the velocities $(1/2,0)$ in $L$ but assigns $(-1/2,0)$ to the
negative and positive particles, respectively, in $R$. After the second
transport, the particle positions in identity order are
\[
 L:(0.00225,0),\qquad R:(0.00025,-0.0025).
\]
At the probe $x_*=0.00125$, the reconstructed fields are $-1/2$ and $0$.

Now restore the Gaussian displacements with $\sigma=10^{-6}$. On the event
that each of the four displacements of a trajectory has modulus at most
$\delta=10^{-4}$, neither its second transport ordering nor its terminal
probe classification changes. The relevant ordering gap is $0.00025>2\delta$
and the smallest probe margin is $0.001>2\delta$. Hence the same field values
hold on this event. For either initial state the exceptional probability is
at most $\epsilon=8\Phi(-100)<\exp(-5003)$. Since every field value is in
$[-1/2,1/2]$,
\[
 \E U_R(x_*,2h)-\E U_L(x_*,2h)\ge\tfrac12-\tfrac32\epsilon>0.
\]
The initial movement of a positive mass to the right thus increases the
expected terminal field. This proves the claim in Remark~\ref{rem:mono}
without inferring an expectation from a finite set of simulation seeds.

\section{Test Problems, Initialization and Reference Solutions}
\label{app:setup}

\paragraph{Initialization.}
For smooth profiles with equal far-field values, let
\[
 w_+(x)=\max(u_0'(x),0),\qquad
 w_-(x)=\max(-u_0'(x),0).
\]
Both have integral $\mathrm{TV}(u_0)/2$. Their normalized cumulative
distributions are
\[
 F_\pm(x)=\frac{2}{\mathrm{TV}(u_0)}\int_{-\infty}^x w_\pm(y)\,\mathrm dy.
\]
For each sign class, we place $N/2$ particles at midpoint quantiles,
\[
 r_k=\frac{k-\tfrac12}{N/2},\qquad
 X^0_{k,\pm}=F_\pm^{-1}(r_k),\qquad k=1,\ldots,N/2,
\]
where $F_\pm^{-1}$ denotes the generalized inverse. The assigned masses are
$\pm\mathrm{TV}(u_0)/N$. This deterministic placement makes the initial state
identical across replicas and comparison arms. For the main two-pulse
experiments, the code approximates the cumulative integrals by the trapezoid
rule on $400\,001$ points in $[-12,12]$ and inverts them by linear
interpolation. It assigns the same absolute mass to both sign classes.

The profile/time study uses a separate numerical initialization on
$200\,001$ points in $[-6,6]$. It differentiates the sampled initial profile,
forms cumulative rectangle sums of its positive and negative parts, and
inverts each cumulative sum by linear interpolation. If $M_+$ and $M_-$
are those two numerical integrals, the assigned masses are
\[
 m_{k,+}=\frac{M_+}{N/2},\qquad
 m_{k,-}=-\frac{M_-}{N/2}.
\]
The reconstruction constant is $u_0(-6)$. This construction has equal counts
per sign class but does not enforce equality of $M_+$ and $M_-$. For the
oscillatory profile, its total signed mass is approximately
$-2.34\times10^{-7}$. The profile/time comparisons are numerical comparisons
of these discrete initial states, and do not invoke the zero-mass identity
for the complete evolution.

\paragraph{Profiles.}
The smooth initial profiles are given in \eqref{eq:twopulse} and \eqref{eq:profiles}.
The reported sign-region counts use a $40\,001$-point evaluation of $u_0'$
and a relative magnitude threshold of $10^{-6}$. This gives $2$, $3$, and
$13$ regions for the bump, two-pulse, and oscillatory profiles. The counts
refer to gradients above the numerical threshold, rather than to every zero
of the analytic derivative on the real line.

The prescribed signed-particle configurations in Section~\ref{sec:structure}
use $N$ particles. The interleaved case alternates masses $\pm1/N$ on
$[-0.25,0.25]$. The separated case places positive masses on $[-2.0,-1.4]$
and negative masses on $[1.4,2.0]$. The merging case uses the intervals
$[-0.9,-0.5]$ and $[0.5,0.9]$. In the latter two cases there are $N/2$
particles of each sign, with magnitude $1/N$. The one-sign control instead
assigns mass $-2/N$ to every particle on $[-1,1]$.

\paragraph{References.} For $f(u)=u^2/2$ and $f(u)=u^3/3$ the reference is a
Fourier pseudospectral solution on $[-16,16]$ with $M=2048$ modes,
$\tfrac23$-rule dealiasing and integrating-factor RK4 at $\mathrm dt=10^{-4}$.
For Burgers, the Cole--Hopf transformation supplies an independent
whole-line reference \citep{hopf1950,cole1951}. With
\[
 F(y)=\int_0^y u_0(z)\,\mathrm dz,
\]
the transformed heat solution and the recovered field are
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \phi(x,t)&=\frac1{\sqrt{4\pi\nu t}}
   \int_\R\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-y)^2}{4\nu t}-\frac{F(y)}{2\nu}\right)
   \,\mathrm dy,\\
 u(x,t)&=-2\nu\,\frac{\partial_x\phi(x,t)}{\phi(x,t)}.
 \end{aligned}
\]
The potential $F$ is analytic for the Gaussian pulses. A stabilized
evaluation agrees with the spectral solution to $5.57\times10^{-14}$ in
maximum norm at the reconstruction points. This is a numerical consistency
check, not a certified quadrature error bound. For the cubic flux, halving
$\mathrm dt$ and doubling $M$ changes the solution by $1.6\times10^{-13}$.
These reference calculations assess field error. They are not timed
competitors and do not enter the fit of a control coefficient.

\section{Additional Estimator Constructions}
\label{app:comparators}

This appendix specifies the supporting estimators used in
Sections~\ref{sec:test2} and~\ref{sec:heldoutA}.

\subsection{Conditional Evaluation and Known-Mean Controls}

For conditional evaluation of the last diffusion stage, let $(a_i,m_i)$ be
one replica's pre-final configuration and let $\Phi$ denote the standard
normal cumulative distribution function. Then
\[
 \E\bigl[\ind\{a_i+\sigma Z_i\le x\}\mid\Fil_{K-1}\bigr]
 =\Phi\!\left(\frac{x-a_i}{\sigma}\right).
\]
The conditional field is therefore
\[
 u_-+\sum_i m_i\Phi\!\left(\frac{x-a_i}{\sigma}\right),
\]
and we average this expression over the two replicas. This operation
integrates out the final indicator noise, rather than replacing a random
displacement by zero.

The heat control and frozen-velocity control use the Brownian displacement
$W_i$ accumulated by each particle identity. Their terminal particle
positions are, respectively,
\[
 X_{i,H}=X_i^0+W_i,\qquad
 X_{i,F}=X_i^0+v_i^0T+W_i.
\]
Here $v_i^0$ is the deterministic initial rank velocity, held fixed in the
surrogate. The cumulative fields built from these positions have known means
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \mu_H(x)&=u_-+\sum_i m_i\Phi\!\left(\frac{x-X_i^0}{\sqrt{2\nu T}}\right),\\
 \mu_F(x)&=u_-+\sum_i m_i\Phi\!\left(\frac{x-X_i^0-v_i^0T}{\sqrt{2\nu T}}\right).
 \end{aligned}
\]
To obtain these means, observe that the predictable sign and permutation
transforms preserve the conditional Gaussian law of every fresh increment.
Each identity's accumulated displacement consequently has distribution
$\mathcal N(0,2\nu T)$.

Writing $\bar H$ for the average heat-control field of the two replicas gives
the control estimator
\[
 \bar U_c(x)=\bar U(x)-c\bigl(\bar H(x)-\mu_H(x)\bigr).
\]
The frozen-velocity estimator is defined in the same way. We examine $c=1$
and a fitted scalar coefficient. On a disjoint training sample, the latter is
\[
 c^*=\frac{\sum_\ell\widehat{\operatorname{Cov}}
       (\bar U(x_\ell),\bar H(x_\ell))}
      {\sum_\ell\widehat{\Var}(\bar H(x_\ell))}.
\]
This minimizes the sample integrated variance over scalar coefficients.
The common factor $\Delta x$ cancels between numerator and denominator.
We hold the fitted coefficient fixed during evaluation and report its
training cost separately.

\subsection{Randomized Quasi-Monte Carlo Implementation}
\label{app:rqmc}

One \texttt{scipy.stats.qmc.Sobol} engine with $d=2$ and $30$ bits is created per
replicate and scrambled once. Successive time steps consume successive blocks of
$N$ points, as in his equation~(45). SciPy's \texttt{scramble=True} is a
left-matrix scramble combined with a digital random shift, not fully nested Owen
scrambling \citep{owen1998,virtanen2020}. The first coordinate assigns a rank by
$\lfloor N p_1\rfloor$ and the second supplies the increment
$\sigma\Phi^{-1}(p_2)$, with $p_2$ clipped to $[2^{-53},1-2^{-53}]$ and the clip
count recorded. $N$ must be a power of two. We do not claim this adaptation
preserves the interacting discrete law. Its total error is measured
independently, including its own bias. The rank-bijection assertion is checked outside the timed path.


\section{Uncertainty Methodology and its Calibration}
\label{app:uncertainty}

\paragraph{Sample quantities and bootstrap intervals.}
For $n$ independent pair fields, let $\langle\bar U\rangle$ denote their
sample mean. The estimates corresponding to \eqref{eq:decomposition} are
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \widehat V&=\frac1{n-1}\sum_{r=1}^n
   \bigl\|\bar U^{(r)}-\langle\bar U\rangle\bigr\|_\Omega^2,\\
 \widehat{b^2}&=\bigl\|\langle\bar U\rangle-u_{\mathrm{ref}}\bigr\|_\Omega^2
   -\frac{\widehat V}{n}.
 \end{aligned}
\]
The subtraction removes the expected sampling contribution to the squared
error of the estimated mean. The resulting estimate can be negative even
though the population squared bias is nonnegative. We retain its sign in
the planning analysis rather than replacing it by zero.

To form a bootstrap interval \citep{efron1981}, we draw $n$ realization
indices with replacement and recompute the complete statistic, including
both parts of any variance ratio. A paired comparison uses the same indices
for its arms. Independent arms are resampled independently. The reported
percentile interval consists of the $2.5$th and $97.5$th percentiles of the
resampled statistic, with resample counts given in Section~\ref{sec:setup}.

\paragraph{Intervals for held-out error.}
Let $e_r$ be the integrated squared error of the complete estimator in block
$r$, for $r=1,\ldots,32$. We report the mean $\bar e$ and use the upper end
of a Bonferroni-adjusted two-sided $t$ interval,
\[
 U=\bar e+t_{31,\,1-\alpha/(2J)}\frac{s_e}{\sqrt{32}},
 \qquad \alpha=0.05,
\]
where $s_e$ is the sample standard deviation of the block errors and $J$
is the number of arms in that experiment. Comparing $U$ with the target
assesses expected error under this interval approximation. It does not
give the probability that an individual execution falls below the target.

\paragraph{Scalar variance-ratio calibration.}
The two interval procedures used for scalar observables are a whole-replicate
percentile bootstrap and $F(n-1,n-1)$. The second assumes independent normal
replicates, which we do not assert, so we calibrated both.

Per-replicate values of four observables were taken from the archived $N=8192$
fields, separately for the reflected and sign-switched arms, and each
sample was centered and scaled by its own standard deviation to give two
empirical shapes. A synthetic numerator arm resamples the sign-switched shape,
scaled to impose a known variance ratio $r$. The denominator arm resamples the
reflected shape. Nominal-$95\%$ intervals are then formed by each procedure and
coverage counted over $M=1500$ synthetic datasets, with $n=48$ per arm and $400$
bootstrap resamples per interval. Table~\ref{tab:calibration} reports the
two-shape design together with the common-shape design of an earlier
calibration, which used one standardized sample for both arms and therefore
does not retain the observed difference between the two distributions.
