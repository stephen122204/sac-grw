\begin{abstract}
Signed gradient-particle methods recover a field by cumulatively summing
positive and negative particle masses. Averaging repeated simulations reduces
sampling error, but the benefit of pairing their random increments depends on
both the mass signs and the particle positions. We study a coupling that matches
two simulations by spatial rank and reflects their Gaussian increments when
the matched masses have the same sign. Opposite-sign masses instead receive
the same increment. The construction specializes established coupling
principles while preserving the probability law of each simulation. We derive
an exact reduction in conditional integrated variance for the final diffusion
stage, with contributions determined by mass products and particle separations.
We then compare the complete nonlinear evolution with reflected pairing,
within-sign pairing, control variates, and a randomized quasi-Monte Carlo
adaptation. Viscous Burgers and cubic-flux tests show an additional reduction
in field variance over within-sign pairing. Separate experiments with plans
selected before evaluation show lower execution time at specified
expected-error targets. Heat and one-sign controls distinguish the effect of
the coupling from nonlinear transport. The benefit depends on the initial
profile, observation time, and requested observable, and decreases as the
common discretization bias dominates. These results support sign-aware spatial
matching for repeated estimation of transient fields by one-dimensional signed
gradient-particle solvers.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Gradient-particle methods approximate the spatial derivative of a solution by a
signed particle measure and recover the field by cumulative summation. This
representation concentrates particles near steep gradients and supports a
mesh-free field reconstruction. Gradient random walks have been developed for
reaction--diffusion and viscous conservation equations
\citep{ghoniem1985,shermanpeskin1986,shermanpeskin1988,roberts1989,shermanmascagni1994}.
More recently, Bertaglia, Pareschi and Caflisch~\citep{bertaglia2024} extended gradient-based Monte Carlo
constructions to relaxation approximations of hyperbolic conservation laws,
including systems. The present work concerns repeated estimation of a transient
field within an existing one-dimensional signed-particle solver.

The computational question is how to couple the randomness in two replicas.
Independent averaging reduces sampling variance but requires additional
trajectories. Antithetic sampling instead seeks a negative covariance between
the outputs of two runs with unchanged marginal laws
\citep{hammersley1956,glasserman2004}. In a signed cumulative reconstruction,
opposite masses reverse the response to a particle displacement. Reflection
of all matched Gaussian increments therefore need not give a favorable field
covariance, even when the particles are close in space.

We match particles by spatial rank and reflect their increments when the
matched masses have the same sign. When their signs differ, we use synchronous
increments. A natural alternative pairs particles within
each sign class and reflects every matched increment. The comparison between
these two sign-aware rules is central. It separates the benefit of retaining
spatial matching from the benefit already obtained by respecting mass signs.

The coupling principles have established antecedents.
N{\"u}sken and Pavliotis~\citep{nusken2018} formulate variance reduction through couplings with preserved
marginals. Their Proposition 54 and Corollaries 57--58 include
reflection/synchronization switches based on products of response-related
quantities. Section 5.2 describes repeated sorting and pairwise matching within
a collection of sampler copies. Their asymptotic variance analysis motivates
such constructions, but does not provide a finite-time variance comparison for
the signed interacting solver studied here. The choice of spatial rank and
mass sign is our specific realization of these ideas for a cumulative field
estimator. It requires no auxiliary equation, although computable switching
criteria also occur in the cited special cases.

Within the gradient-particle literature, L{\'e}cot~\citep{lecot_rims2001} combines signed
cumulative reconstruction, spatial ordering, and quasi-random diffusion inputs
for a Burgers construction. A related treatment appears in
L{\'e}cot and Ogawa~\citep{lecot2002}. This is the closest representation-specific precedent for
structuring the random inputs. Our randomized quasi-Monte Carlo comparison
adapts that diffusion strategy to the transport update used here. It is not a
reproduction of the complete published algorithm.

Recent QMC particle constructions also couple noise within an interacting
ensemble. Ben Rached et al.~\citep{benrached2024} prove weak convergence for additive-noise
McKean--Vlasov dynamics and develop an antithetic multilevel QMC estimator.
Their construction and regularity assumptions differ from our signed
rank-dependent solver. Together with array-RQMC work for a Fokker--Planck
particle model \citep{netterdon2026}, this places the diffusion adaptation below
within a broader literature. Its result cannot rank QMC methods as a family.

Bencheikh and Jourdain~\citep{bencheikhjourdain2019} study bias and antithetic sampling for mean-field
particle approximations. Their equation (3.1) couples a $2N$-particle system
with two $N$-particle systems to form a particle-number correction, an object
different from our same-size replica average. Related convergence and bias
analyses \citep{bossytalay1997,bossy2004,bossyjourdain2002,jourdain2000,bencheikh2020}
concern particular underlying particle schemes and assumptions. Preservation
of our baseline's law does not extend those theorems to a different
reconstruction or transport convention.

Variance reduction can also act through a control variate, conditional
expectation, or a modified particle representation
\citep{glasserman2004,baker2005,homolle2007,almohssen2010}.
We compare the coupling with two known-mean controls and conditional evaluation
of the last diffusion stage. These comparisons concern the selected estimators
and their implementations, rather than the best attainable performance of each
variance-reduction family.

The contribution has three components. First, we specify a sign-aware
spatial-rank coupling for signed cumulative field estimation and establish
preservation of each replica's complete discrete law. This retains the
baseline expectation and allows its discretization bias to be studied
separately from the covariance introduced by pairing.

Second, we derive an exact final-stage variance identity and identify an
unconditional equality case when all masses have one sign. The identity is
local to the conditioned diffusion stage. A counterexample shows why an
unrestricted coordinatewise-monotonicity argument cannot supply the missing
nonlinear terminal comparison.

Third, we quantify the additional computational benefit over within-sign
pairing, alongside reflected sampling, controls, and a randomized
quasi-Monte Carlo adaptation. Fixed-profile experiments, two distinct held-out
target comparisons, and nonlinear observables establish the empirical scope.

The intended use is ensemble field estimation after a signed-particle solver
has been selected. We do not compare its efficiency with deterministic PDE
solvers. The computations are one-dimensional and scalar, and use
the mean-transport update defined below. In particular it does not establish
an advantage for sampled two-speed transport.

Section~\ref{sec:method} defines the update and estimators.
Section~\ref{sec:theory} gives their exact properties.
Section~\ref{sec:numerics} presents the comparisons and applicability tests,
followed by the discussion and conclusions. Appendices supply the proofs,
reference and initialization details, supporting estimator constructions,
and statistical methodology.

\section{The solver, the estimators and the coupling}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Problem class and signed particle representation}

We consider the scalar viscous conservation law
\begin{equation}
 \partial_t u+\partial_x f(u)=\nu\partial_{xx}u,\qquad
 u(x,0)=u_0(x),\quad x\in\R,\quad 0<t\le T.
 \label{eq:pde}
\end{equation}
We take $f\in C^1(\R)$ and $\nu>0$, with initial data $u_0$ of
bounded variation whose derivative is integrable and whose limits
$u_\pm=\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}u_0(x)$ exist. The experiments use
$f(u)=u^2/2$ (viscous Burgers), $f(u)=u^3/3$, and $f\equiv 0$ (heat), the last
as a control in which the coupling acts without transport.

For a smooth solution, differentiating \eqref{eq:pde} gives
\[
 \partial_t w+\partial_x\bigl(f'(u)w\bigr)=\nu\partial_{xx}w,
 \qquad w=\partial_x u.
\]
Thus the gradient is transported with velocity $f'(u)$ and diffused with
coefficient $\nu$. The signed-gradient representation used in random-walk
methods approximates $w$ by weighted point masses and recovers $u$ by
integration (see \citep{ghoniem1985,roberts1989,lecot_rims2001}). We write
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
  w^N(\cdot,t)&=\sum_{i=1}^N m_i\,\delta_{X_i(t)},\\
  U^N(x,t)&=u_-+\!\!\sum_{i:\,X_i(t)\le x}\!\! m_i .
 \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:representation}
\end{equation}
Here $\delta_{X_i}$ is the Dirac measure at particle position $X_i$, and
$m_i$ is its signed mass. We keep the masses fixed and evolve the positions.
For smooth profiles with equal far-field values, the positive and negative
parts of $u_0'$ have equal integral. The main two-pulse experiments place
$N/2$ particles at midpoint quantiles of each part and assign magnitude
\[
 |m_i|=\frac{\mathrm{TV}(u_0)}{N},\qquad
 \mathrm{TV}(u_0)=\int_\R |u_0'(x)|\,\mathrm dx.
\]
Here $\mathrm{TV}(u_0)$ is the total variation of the initial field. Equal
counts and equal magnitudes enforce $\sum_i m_i=0$ for this construction.
The profile/time study instead normalizes each sign class using its sampled
integral, and the one-sign control has nonzero total mass. These numerical
initializations are specified in Appendix~\ref{app:setup}. Zero total mass
is required for the whole-line product identity in Lemma~\ref{lem:wholeline},
but not for preservation of a replica's law.

\subsection{One step of a single replica}

The diffusion stage uses the Gaussian random-walk increment of variance
$2\nu h$ \citep{ghoniem1985}. We fix a time step $h>0$ and set
\[
 K=T/h\in\mathbb N,\qquad \sigma=\sqrt{2\nu h}.
\]
A replica (i.e., one simulation of the particle system) keeps
a state $(X,m,v)$ of positions, masses and carried velocities. Its storage
order is the most recent pre-diffusion rank order. After diffusion the positions
need not be sorted. Algorithm~\ref{alg:single} specifies the update used in all comparisons.
We first transport with the carried velocity and sort the particles. We then
evaluate the new velocity from the cumulative mass at each rank, including
that particle's own mass, and apply diffusion. This is our baseline convention.
It is not a reproduction of the shock and rarefaction transport rules in
L{\'e}cot~\citep{lecot_rims2001}.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{One step of a single replica (mean-transport gradient random walk)}
\label{alg:single}
\SetAlgoLined
\KwIn{state $(X,m,v)$ with associated entries, step $h$, viscosity $\nu$, flux derivative $f'$}
$X_i \leftarrow X_i + v_i\,h$ \qquad \tcp*[h]{transport with the carried velocity}\;
$\pi\leftarrow$ a stable sort of $X$; \quad $(X,m)\leftarrow(X_\pi,m_\pi)$\;
$v_i \leftarrow f'\!\bigl(u_- + \sum_{j\le i} m_j\bigr)$
  \tcp*{new carried velocity, inclusive cumulative sum}
draw $Z\sim\mathcal N(0,I_N)$ independent of the past;
  \quad $X_i \leftarrow X_i + \sigma Z_i$\;
\end{algorithm}

Three features of Algorithm~\ref{alg:single} matter later. The velocity is
computed after the sort and before the diffusion, and is used by
the next transport, so it is part of the state and must stay associated with its particle until
used. At the next sort, we discard and recompute that velocity. Any other
reordering must permute the velocity together with the position and mass.
Particles at the same position retain their previous relative order. We do not sort again between diffusion and the next transport.

The velocity is evaluated from the rank-inclusive cumulative sum. At tied
positions this convention uses the stable particle order. It differs from the value
of a field reconstruction that includes all co-located masses at once. The
same convention is used in every coupling. This mean-transport baseline uses the reconstructed velocity directly,
without sampling a velocity label.

\subsection{Two replicas, a matching, and a multiplier}

Reflection and synchronization are established ways to couple Gaussian
increments \citep{hammersley1956,nusken2018}. Reflection gives the partners
opposite increments, whereas synchronization gives them the same increment.
The sign-dependent switches in N{\"u}sken and Pavliotis~\citep[Proposition 54 and Corollaries 57--58]{nusken2018}
motivate this choice. Here we specify the matching and switching rule for
signed cumulative reconstruction.

Both replicas start from the same deterministic initial state. They are advanced
in lockstep. At stage $k$ let
\[
  S_k=\bigl(X^A,m^A,v^A,X^B,m^B,v^B\bigr)
\]
be the joint state after the sort and the velocity update of
Algorithm~\ref{alg:single} and before the diffusion. The information available at that stage is denoted by $\Fil_k$ and includes
the complete joint history and all previous random draws. A rule measurable
with respect to $\Fil_k$ therefore uses only information available before the
fresh diffusion draw.

\begin{definition}[admissible coupling]
\label{def:coupling}
An \emph{admissible coupling} is a rule that, at each stage $k$, selects a
bijection $\pi_k:\{1,\dots,N\}\to\{1,\dots,N\}$ and multipliers
$\varepsilon_k\in\{-1,+1\}^N$, both $\Fil_k$-measurable, draws
$Z^{(k)}\sim\mathcal N(0,I_N)$ independent of $\Fil_k$ and of all earlier draws,
and sets
\begin{equation}
  X^A_i \leftarrow X^A_i+\sigma Z^{(k)}_i,
  \qquad
  X^B_j \leftarrow X^B_j+\sigma\,\varepsilon_{k,j}\,Z^{(k)}_{\pi_k(j)} .
  \label{eq:coupling}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

Three admissible couplings are compared throughout. All three use the same
$Z^{(k)}$ for replica $A$. They differ only in $(\pi_k,\varepsilon_k)$.

\begin{description}
\item[\refl{} (reflected pairing).] $\pi_k=\mathrm{id}$ and
  $\varepsilon_{k,j}=-1$. Matched by spatial rank, antithetic increments. This
  is the natural default.
\item[\swi{} (sign-switched pairing), the construction studied here.]
  $\pi_k=\mathrm{id}$ and
  \begin{equation}
    \varepsilon_{k,j}=-\,\sgn\!\bigl(m^A_j\bigr)\,\sgn\!\bigl(m^B_j\bigr),
    \label{eq:switch}
  \end{equation}
  so a matched pair with like masses is reflected and a matched pair with
  unlike masses is synchronised.
\item[\wit{} (within-sign pairing).] $\pi_k$ maps the $r$-th particle of sign
  $s$ in $B$ to the $r$-th particle of sign $s$ in $A$, for $s\in\{+,-\}$, and
  $\varepsilon_{k,j}=-1$. No matched pair ever has unlike masses.
\end{description}

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Coupled advance of two replicas}
\label{alg:pair}
\SetAlgoLined
\KwIn{common initial state $(X^0,m^0)$ sorted by position, $u_-$, steps $K$,
      step $h$, viscosity $\nu$, flux derivative $f'$, policy
      $\in\{\refl,\swi,\wit\}$}
$(X^A,m^A)\leftarrow(X^0,m^0)$; \quad $(X^B,m^B)\leftarrow(X^0,m^0)$;
  \quad $\sigma\leftarrow\sqrt{2\nu h}$\;
$v^A_i\leftarrow f'\!\bigl(u_-+\sum_{j\le i}m^A_j\bigr)$; \quad $v^B\leftarrow v^A$\;
\For{$k=0,\dots,K-1$}{
  \ForEach{replica $Q\in\{A,B\}$}{
    $X^Q_i\leftarrow X^Q_i+v^Q_ih$ \tcp*{transport}
    stable-sort $(X^Q,m^Q)$ by $X^Q$\;
    $v^Q_i\leftarrow f'\!\bigl(u_-+\sum_{j\le i}m^Q_j\bigr)$
      \tcp*{velocity for the next transport}
  }
  \uIf{policy $=\refl$}{$\pi\leftarrow\mathrm{id}$; \quad $\varepsilon_j\leftarrow-1$\;}
  \uElseIf{policy $=\swi$}{$\pi\leftarrow\mathrm{id}$; \quad
    $\varepsilon_j\leftarrow-\sgn(m^A_j)\sgn(m^B_j)$\;}
  \Else(\tcp*[h]{$\wit$}){\ForEach{$s\in\{+1,-1\}$}{
      $I_s\leftarrow\{i:\sgn(m^A_i)=s\}$, $J_s\leftarrow\{j:\sgn(m^B_j)=s\}$,
        both in increasing order\;
      assert $|I_s|=|J_s|$; $\pi(J_s[r])\leftarrow I_s[r]$ for $r=1,\dots,|I_s|$;
        \quad $\varepsilon\leftarrow-1$ on those $j$\;
      }}
  draw $Z\sim\mathcal N(0,I_N)$ \tcp*{fresh, independent of the joint history}
  $X^A_i\leftarrow X^A_i+\sigma Z_i$; \quad
  $X^B_j\leftarrow X^B_j+\sigma\varepsilon_jZ_{\pi(j)}$\;
}
\KwOut{$(X^A,m^A)$, $(X^B,m^B)$; fields by \eqref{eq:representation} and the
       pair mean by \eqref{eq:est-pair}}
\end{algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:pair} is implemented by the shared coupling stepper in the
accompanying repository. Its Burgers trajectories are checked against the
conditional-mean baseline using prescribed draws. Drawing the fresh vector
before or after evaluating the state-dependent rule gives the same algorithm
provided the rule does not inspect that vector.

Figure~\ref{fig:construction} shows the two matchings on one paired state.
Within-sign matching restricts each particle to a partner of the same sign,
which can increase the separation. Spatial-rank matching pairs the $i$th
particle of one replica with the $i$th of the other and uses the mass signs
to choose the increment orientation.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig1_construction.pdf}
\caption{The two matchings applied to the same paired state immediately before
the tenth diffusion stage of the interleaved-sign configuration, with $N=12$.
The left panel pairs particles of equal spatial rank and synchronizes the
increments of unlike-sign pairs. The right panel restricts partners to the
same sign and reflects every pair. The state is identical in both panels,
so the difference in separation comes only from the matching rule.
Both rules preserve each replica's law (Proposition~\ref{prop:law}).}

\label{fig:construction}
\end{figure}


The Gaussian vector must be independent of the complete joint history, and
the matching must be a bijection. Reusing one coordinate for two particles of
the same replica changes their joint transition law. The multipliers must
equal $+1$ or $-1$ to preserve the increment variance. We exclude zero masses,
since inserting $\sgn(0)=0$ into \eqref{eq:switch} would suppress diffusion
for that particle. An implementation retaining zero masses would need an
explicit convention, such as setting the multiplier to $-1$.

Within-sign matching also requires equal counts in each sign class across the
replicas. Our deterministic common initialization and fixed masses ensure
this property. These conditions, together with retaining the association
between each particle and its carried velocity, specify the full transition
whose law is preserved below.

\subsection{Estimators, error functional and work}
\label{sec:estimators}

Write $U^{(A)},U^{(B)}$ for the two reconstructed fields at time $T$.
The unpaired estimator averages $B$ independent simulations,
\begin{equation}
 \hat u^{\mathrm{sing}}_B(x)=\frac1B\sum_{b=1}^{B}U^{(b)}(x).
 \label{eq:est-single}
\end{equation}
For coupled simulations we first average the two fields in each pair,
\begin{equation}
 \bar U^{(b)}(x)=\tfrac12\bigl(U^{(A,b)}(x)+U^{(B,b)}(x)\bigr),
 \label{eq:est-pair}
\end{equation}
and then average $B$ independent realizations of the pair,
\begin{equation}
 \hat u_B(x)=\frac1B\sum_{b=1}^{B}\bar U^{(b)}(x).
 \label{eq:est-ens}
\end{equation}
Thus $B$ counts single simulations in \eqref{eq:est-single} and pairs in
\eqref{eq:est-ens}. A paired estimate requires $2B$ trajectories.

We reconstruct the fields at $L=400$ equispaced points $x_\ell$ in
$\Omega=[-5,5]$, with spacing $\Delta x$. The discrete approximation of
integrated squared error is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal E(B)=\E\bigl\|\hat u_B-u_{\mathrm{ref}}\bigr\|_\Omega^2,
 \qquad
 \|g\|_\Omega^2=\Delta x\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}g(x_\ell)^2.
 \label{eq:error}
\end{equation}
Here $u_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is an independently computed spectral or Cole--Hopf
solution, specified in Appendix~\ref{app:setup}. Independence across
replicates gives the bias--variance decomposition
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal E(B)=b^2+\frac{V}{B},
 \label{eq:decomposition}
\end{equation}
where the squared bias and integrated variance are
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 b^2&=\bigl\|\E\bar U-u_{\mathrm{ref}}\bigr\|_\Omega^2,\\
 V&=\Delta x\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\Var\bigl(\bar U(x_\ell)\bigr).
 \end{aligned}
\]
The bias includes finite-particle and time-stepping errors, together with any
error in the reference. Only the variance term decreases when more independent
pairs are averaged at fixed particle count and time step.

We measure computational work by elapsed execution time,
\[
 W=BC,
\]
where $C$ is the measured cost of one replicate. Each experiment states its
timing protocol. The cost of selecting a plan from preliminary simulations
is reported separately from execution of that plan.

The discrete norm in \eqref{eq:error} is used for the numerical comparisons.
The theoretical identity in Section~\ref{sec:theory} instead integrates over
the whole real line. For nonlinear observables, applying a functional to the
pair mean and averaging its values on the two fields give different
estimators. Their expectations and total errors are compared in
Section~\ref{sec:qD}.

\section{Properties of the coupled estimator}
\label{sec:theory}

Law preservation applies to the full discrete trajectory. The variance identity
below instead conditions on a single paired state immediately before diffusion.
This distinction is necessary because earlier coupling choices change the state
on which later stages act.

\subsection{Each replica keeps its law}

\begin{proposition}[marginal law preservation]
\label{prop:law}
Let two replicas be advanced by Algorithm~\ref{alg:single} from the same initial
state under any admissible coupling (Definition~\ref{def:coupling}). Then the
law of the complete state trajectory
$\bigl(X^A_k,m^A_k,v^A_k\bigr)_{k=0}^{K}$ of replica $A$, and likewise of
replica $B$, is exactly that of an uncoupled single replica. Only the joint law
of the two changes.
\end{proposition}

This argument uses invariance of the Gaussian distribution, as do the adapted
orthogonal transformations in N{\"u}sken and Pavliotis~\citep[Appendix A]{nusken2018}. In our proof
(Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}), a predictable permutation
of an i.i.d.\ Gaussian vector is i.i.d.\ Gaussian, and predictable $\pm1$
multipliers preserve $\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$ coordinatewise, so each replica's
one-step transition kernel is unchanged given the past.

\begin{remark}[dependence on the baseline update]
\label{rem:hyp}
The proposition requires the conditional Gaussian transition and replica-local
deterministic update specified above. In particular it does
not need the particle signs to be constant in time, nor the output
functional to be linear in the particle measure. The present solver keeps signs fixed. A scheme in
which masses were updated would still preserve each replica's law provided the
updates are functions of that replica's own state and the multipliers remain
predictable and of unit modulus.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}[shared bias]
\label{cor:bias}
Under any admissible coupling, $\E\bar U=\E U^{\mathrm{sing}}$ pointwise,
provided the displayed expectations exist. Thus the squared bias in
\eqref{eq:decomposition} is identical for \refl{}, \swi{}, and \wit{} under the
same baseline initialization and update.
\end{corollary}

The conclusion follows by linearity of expectation over the two replicas.
More generally it holds for the average of any integrable per-replica
functional. It does not hold automatically for a nonlinear function of the pair
mean. Preservation of the baseline law retains its own valid expectation and
bias results, but it neither establishes continuum convergence nor imports
error rates proved for other particle methods.

\subsection{An exact identity for the final diffusion stage}

We express the integrated covariance of cumulative fields in terms of
particle separations. The following product identity supplies that step.
Its zero-mass assumption ensures that the fields vanish outside a bounded
interval, so their product is integrable over the real line.

\begin{lemma}[whole-line product identity]
\label{lem:wholeline}
Let $D_A(x)=\sum_i m^A_i\ind\{X^A_i\le x\}$ and
$D_B(x)=\sum_j m^B_j\ind\{X^B_j\le x\}$ with
$\sum_i m^A_i=\sum_j m^B_j=0$. Then $D_A,D_B$ are compactly supported and
\begin{equation}
  \int_{\R}D_A(x)\,D_B(x)\,\mathrm dx
  \;=\;-\tfrac12\sum_{i,j}m^A_i\,m^B_j\,\bigl|X^A_i-X^B_j\bigr| .
  \label{eq:wholeline}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proposition}[final-stage identity]
\label{prop:finalstage}
Let $g_s(d)=\E|d+sZ|$ with $Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$, and condition on the paired
state immediately before the last diffusion stage, with $a_i,b_i$ the positions
of the rank-$i$ particles of $A$ and $B$, $d_i=a_i-b_i$, and masses
$m^A_i,m^B_i$ satisfying the hypothesis of Lemma~\ref{lem:wholeline}. Write
$V_{\!\bullet}=\int_{\R}\Var\bigl(\bar U(x)\mid \text{state}\bigr)\mathrm dx$
for the conditional whole-line integrated variance of the pair mean under
coupling $\bullet$. Then
\begin{equation}
  V_{\refl}-V_{\swi}
  \;=\;\frac14\!\!\sum_{i:\,m^A_i m^B_i<0}\!\!
    \bigl|m^A_i m^B_i\bigr|\,
    \Bigl[g_{2\sigma}(d_i)-|d_i|\Bigr]\;\ge\;0 .
  \label{eq:finalstage}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

Only matched pairs of unlike mass contribute. Each term is proportional to
the product of the mass magnitudes and to the excess of $g_{2\sigma}(d_i)$ over
$|d_i|$, which is largest when the matched separation is small relative to
$\sigma$ and decays as $|d_i|/\sigma$ grows. Non-negativity is Jensen's
inequality. The proof (Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}) uses
Lemma~\ref{lem:wholeline} twice and the observation that all off-diagonal terms
$i\ne j$ are unaffected by the coupling, because the two increments involved are
then independent under the two rank-matched rules being compared.


Equation~\eqref{eq:finalstage} does not say that switching helps whenever the
matched signs disagree at an earlier stage. The state distributions at later
stages are themselves coupling-dependent. The numerical check of the identity is reported in Appendix~\ref{app:finalcheck}. Averaging the right-hand side over reflected histories estimates the reduction
obtained by switching only their last stage. The conditional mean is unchanged,
so the law of total variance gives this comparison exactly. Dividing that
estimated reduction by the separately measured full-history reduction gives
$3.75\%$, $3.71\%$, and $1.95\%$ in three archived Gaussian/two-pulse cells.
These are descriptive ratios of estimates, not a guaranteed fraction of the
full-history gain. The identity therefore explains a local improvement but
leaves most of the measured terminal comparison to numerical investigation.

\subsection{When the two couplings are identical}

\begin{lemma}[conditional identity]
\label{lem:conditional}
Start both couplings from the same initial state and drive them with the same
sequence of draws $Z^{(k)}$. Then their complete states coincide at every stage
through the pre-diffusion state of the first stage at which a matched pair
has unlike masses. Their diffusion updates at that stage can differ. In particular, if no such stage occurs before $K$, the two runs are identical.
\end{lemma}

While $\sgn(m^A_i)\sgn(m^B_i)=+1$ for all $i$, the
multiplier \eqref{eq:switch} equals $-1$, which is the reflected multiplier. Induction over the stages gives the claim.

\begin{corollary}[one-sign case]
\label{cor:onesign}
If every gradient mass has the same sign, the hypothesis of
Lemma~\ref{lem:conditional} holds for every history, and \swi{} and \refl{}
are the same algorithm.
\end{corollary}

For monotone initial data, all gradient masses have the same sign.
Corollary~\ref{cor:onesign} therefore shows that switching makes no change
to reflected pairing in this case.
Reflected pairing itself can still reduce variance relative to independent runs. We
checked equality of positions, masses, and carried velocities for
$200$ seeds under both heat and Burgers dynamics, with maximum absolute
difference exactly $0$ in every seed.

For initially separated signs, the situation is different.
Finite separation of the positive and negative blocks does not ensure equality
of the two couplings. Gaussian increments can reverse their order. For two
particles initially separated by $d>0$, a reflected pair of simulations has
exactly one order reversal with probability
\[
 \Pr\bigl(|Z_1-Z_2|>d/\sigma\bigr)>0.
\]
The resulting mismatch of signs at equal ranks can make subsequent switched
and reflected updates differ. In the separated configuration at $N=2048$,
the complete states diverged in $58/200=0.290$ $[0.228,0.358]$ of fresh seeds
under heat dynamics and $59/200=0.295$ $[0.233,0.363]$ under Burgers.
The brackets are exact binomial intervals. Among the runs that diverged,
the median first divergence occurred at step $187$ of $200$.

\subsection{No unrestricted monotonicity}

\begin{remark}[coordinatewise monotonicity fails]
\label{rem:mono}
Coordinatewise monotonicity of a response is a standard sufficient condition
for antithetic variance reduction \citep{glasserman2004}. The corresponding
orientation of the terminal field in each particle's initial position fails
for Algorithm~\ref{alg:single}. With masses $(1/2,-1/2)$, $h=0.005$ and
$\nu=10^{-10}$, moving the positive particle right from $-0.00275$ to
$-0.00225$, while keeping the negative particle at zero, increases the expected
field at $x=0.00125$ after two steps. Appendix~\ref{app:counterexample} gives
the exact zero-noise trace and a Gaussian-tail bound establishing the strict
inequality for positive viscosity.
\end{remark}

This counterexample excludes the unrestricted monotonicity argument for the
specified discrete update. It does not compare the terminal variances of
\swi{} and \refl{}, and does not exclude a result under additional restrictions on particle
spacing, time step, or viscosity.
Its transport-to-diffusion ratio $|m|h/\sigma=2500$ lies far outside the main
experiments.

\subsection{A geometric fact about rank matching}
\label{sec:geometry}

To compare the geometry of the two matchings, we measure the mean distance
between matched particles at the same paired state. The following standard
property of sorted matching fixes the direction of that comparison.

\begin{remark}[sorted matching is the optimal matching for absolute distance]
\label{rem:sorted}
For two equally sized finite sets of reals $\{a_i\}$ and $\{b_j\}$ listed in
increasing order, the identity bijection minimises
\[
 \sum_i|a_i-b_{\pi(i)}|
\]
over all bijections $\pi$. This is the monotone rearrangement, which is
optimal for convex displacement costs \citep{villani2003}. Rank matching is that bijection, and within-sign
matching is a bijection constrained to respect the sign classes. Hence
\begin{equation*}
  \sum_i\bigl|a_i-b_i\bigr|\;\le\;\sum_i\bigl|a_i-b_{\pi_{\wit}(i)}\bigr|
\end{equation*}
with probability one, and the ratio of mean matched separations reported in
Table~\ref{tab:separation} is bounded above by $1$ by construction.
\end{remark}

The separation ratio measures how much the sign constraint increases the
mean distance between partners. Proposition~\ref{prop:finalstage} relates
distance to the conditional variance reduction for rank matching. It does
not compare rank matching with within-sign matching, whose covariance also
depends on the different partners. We therefore use separation to describe
the geometry, rather than to infer the cause of a terminal variance difference.

\section{Numerical study}

\subsection{Test configuration and statistical intervals}
\label{sec:setup}

Unless stated otherwise the test problem is the asymmetric signed two-pulse
initial condition
\begin{equation}
  u_0(x)=0.8\,e^{-(x+0.7)^2/(2\cdot 0.5^2)}-0.5\,e^{-(x-0.4)^2/(2\cdot 0.3^2)},
  \label{eq:twopulse}
\end{equation}
with $f(u)=u^2/2$, $\nu=0.1$, $T=1$ and $h=0.005$ ($K=200$ steps), evaluated on
$\Omega=[-5,5]$ at $L=400$ points. It has three sign regions in the numerically resolved $u_0'$, so both
sign classes are populated and the classes interleave once. Reference solutions
are Fourier pseudospectral on $[-16,16]$ with $M=2048$ modes, $\tfrac23$
dealiasing and an integrating-factor RK4 step of $10^{-4}$. For Burgers this
agrees with a stabilised whole-line Cole--Hopf evaluation to $5.6\times10^{-14}$
in the maximum norm, and for the cubic flux it self-converges to
$1.6\times10^{-13}$ (Appendix~\ref{app:setup}).

All reported experiments use recorded deterministic seed keys. Independent
replicates use distinct streams. The archive specifies the complete key tuple
for each experiment, including whether the comparison arms share a stream.
Bootstrap resampling uses complete realizations rather than individual
reconstruction points \citep{efron1981}. Comparisons with shared streams
resample the corresponding realizations together, preserving their dependence. The main
field, control and nonlinear-MSE tables use $6000$ percentile-bootstrap
resamples. The profile/time and structure tables retain their archived $3000$
and $2000$ resamples, respectively. Scalar station variance ratios use
$F(n-1,n-1)$ intervals for independent arms. Held-out errors use $t$ intervals
on $32$ independent block errors, with Bonferroni correction across the arms of
each experiment. Divergence frequencies use exact binomial intervals.
The intervals are approximate except where an exact model-based construction
is specified. Appendix~\ref{app:uncertainty} gives an empirical scalar
calibration and its limits. It does not establish coverage for every field or MSE interval.

Throughout, a ratio below $1$ favours the numerator
and an interval containing $1$ means the comparison is unresolved at this
sample size. Such an interval does not establish absence of an effect.

\subsection{Work at selected accuracy targets}
\label{sec:qC}

To measure execution time at a specified accuracy, we selected plans using
preliminary simulations and evaluated them on independent random streams.
For each arm, the preliminary sample supplied estimates of squared bias,
integrated variance, and cost per replicate. Upper interval endpoints
$b^2_{\mathrm{hi}}$ and $V_{\mathrm{hi}}$ determined the smallest positive
integer $B$ satisfying
\[
 b^2_{\mathrm{hi}}+\frac{V_{\mathrm{hi}}}{B}
 \le\frac{\mathrm{tol}}{\kappa},\qquad \kappa=1.3.
\]
The factor $\kappa$ provides planning headroom. Once chosen, each plan was
held fixed and evaluated in $32$ independent blocks, where a block is one
execution of the complete $B$-replicate estimator. At a fixed particle count
and time step, paired and single arms use the same pilot estimate of squared
bias, taken from the switched arm. The randomized quasi-Monte Carlo arm uses
its own estimate because its interacting law differs.

The two experiments use different targets, particle counts, and comparators.
Experiment A includes an unpaired ensemble and a quasi-Monte Carlo adaptation.
Experiment B compares the three pairings, including within-sign matching.

\subsubsection{Experiment A with unpaired and quasi-Monte Carlo estimators}
\label{sec:heldoutA}

Experiment A uses $N=8192$ and targets expected integrated squared error
$7\times10^{-6}$. The pilot and evaluation seed keys are $1601$ and $1702$. The target was chosen from the pilot,
so it is a pilot-informed target with an independent evaluation batch.

The quasi-Monte Carlo arm is an adaptation of the rank-based diffusion
of L{\'e}cot~\citep{lecot_rims2001} to our transport solver, not a reproduction of his method.

\[
 r=\lfloor Np_1\rfloor,\qquad \Delta X=\sigma\Phi^{-1}(p_2).
\]

Appendix~\ref{app:rqmc} specifies the scrambling, endpoint treatment, and
implementation checks. This adaptation does not preserve the baseline's
interacting discrete law, so its total error includes a separately estimated
bias.

\subsubsection{Nonlinear functionals of the reconstructed field}

Let $Q$ be a nonlinear functional of the field. We distinguish
\[
 Q(\bar U)\qquad\text{and}\qquad
 \tfrac12\bigl(Q(U^{(A)})+Q(U^{(B)})\bigr).
\]
The first applies $Q$ after averaging the fields, whereas the second averages
the two values of $Q$. Their expectations can differ. By Proposition~\ref{prop:law} the second has exactly the
expectation of $Q$ applied to a single uncoupled run, so its bias is the
single-run bias under every admissible coupling. The first estimator has no
such guarantee. We
therefore report the empirical mean squared error against the reference,
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\mathrm{MSE}}
  =\frac1n\sum_{r=1}^{n}\bigl(Q_r-Q_{\mathrm{ref}}\bigr)^2
  =\widehat{\mathrm{bias}}^2+\frac{n-1}{n}\,s^2 ,
  \label{eq:empmse}
\end{equation}
where $\widehat{\mathrm{bias}}=n^{-1}\sum_r Q_r-Q_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and
$s^2$ is the sample variance with divisor $n-1$. This differs from the plug-in $\widehat{\mathrm{bias}}^2+s^2$, which exceeds
\eqref{eq:empmse} by $s^2/n$ (here $0.7\%$--$2.0\%$). Table~\ref{tab:nonlinear}
uses \eqref{eq:empmse} throughout. We evaluate two functionals. The maximum
gradient uses central differences at the reconstruction points and depends
on their spacing. The level location is the first downward crossing of
$u=0.25$ to the right of the global maximum, found by linear interpolation.
We record a realization without such a crossing as censored. None occurred
in the reported configurations.

\subsubsection{The bias-dominated regime}

The same arithmetic applies to the field itself. Since the couplings share
$b^2$, \eqref{eq:decomposition} gives
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\mathcal E_{\swi}(B)}{\mathcal E_{\refl}(B)}
  =\frac{b^2+V_{\swi}/B}{b^2+V_{\refl}/B}\;\longrightarrow\;1
  \qquad\text{as } B\to\infty ,
  \label{eq:crossover}
\end{equation}
when $b^2>0$. If $b^2=0$, the ratio instead remains $V_{\swi}/V_{\refl}$.
Thus the crossover concerns nonzero baseline bias at fixed $N$ and $h$. The
crossover occurs at
\[
 B^{\star}=V/b^2,
\]
where the sampling and squared-bias contributions are equal. At three archived cells of the two-pulse
problem $B^{\star}$ for the switched arm is $10.3$ ($N=6400$, $h=0.0025$),
$4.5$ ($N=6400$, $h=0.005$) and $35.3$ ($N=1600$, $h=0.0025$).
Figure~\ref{fig:bias} uses equal continuous work instead of equal pair counts:
\begin{equation}
 \frac{b^2+V_{\swi}C_{\swi}/W}{b^2+V_{\refl}C_{\refl}/W}.
 \label{eq:equalwork}
\end{equation}
This is a plug-in model, excluding integer allocation and timing uncertainty.
The crossover marks equal sampling and bias contributions in the switched arm. It is not a cutoff for improvement.

\section{Discussion and conclusions}
\label{sec:discussion}

Spatial-rank matching with a sign-dependent increment rule reduces the
sampling error of signed cumulative field estimates while preserving each
replica's discrete law. The switching principle and adaptive matching have
precedents in coupled sampling \citep{nusken2018}, and spatial sorting with
structured diffusion inputs is established for gradient particles
\citep{lecot_rims2001}. Our contribution specifies their use for paired
transient field estimation, derives its final-stage variance identity, and
measures the additional benefit over within-sign pairing.

Within-sign pairing accounts for most of the reduction over reflected rank
pairing. Retaining spatial matching gives a further reduction in the principal
two-pulse experiment, with integrated variance $0.739$ times the within-sign
value and integrated mean squared error $0.819$ times as large. In a separate
comparison at a specified expected-error target, the selected switched plan
uses four trajectories against six for within-sign pairing and takes
approximately $0.60$ of its execution time. These plans attain different
errors below the target, so the result describes their measured work rather
than an optimized equal-error comparison.

The exact identity relates the conditional gain to mass magnitudes, signs,
and particle separations at the final diffusion stage. Earlier coupling
choices change the distribution of later states, and the full-history
comparison remains numerical. Applying both matching rules at a common state
shows the additional separation imposed by the within-sign constraint.
That geometric property does not establish the cause of the terminal variance
reduction, since covariance also depends on which particles are paired.

The profile, time, and cubic-flux comparisons describe a benefit beyond one
initial condition. The one-sign identity and unresolved comparisons also
identify limits. Coupling is most useful when sampling contributes materially
to the requested field error. It cannot remove the common baseline bias,
and nonlinear functionals require their own total-error comparisons.
The method is therefore an option for ensemble estimation after a signed
gradient-particle solver has been selected.

The computations use scalar equations in one dimension, constant particle
masses, and mean transport. Sufficient conditions for a terminal variance
comparison under nonlinear evolution remain open. Extending the matching
construction to systems or several dimensions, and evaluating its cost in
parallel implementations, would require separate analysis and experiments.

\section{Proofs}
\label{app:proofs}

\subsection{Proposition~\ref{prop:law}}

Let $\Xi^A_k=(X^A_k,m^A_k,v^A_k)$ be the complete state entering
stage $k$, with $k=0,\ldots,K-1$. Let $\Fil_k$ contain the joint history
through the sort and velocity update of that stage, as in
Definition~\ref{def:coupling}. The deterministic part of
Algorithm~\ref{alg:single} acts on $\Xi^A_k$ alone. We compare the conditional
increment laws before constructing $\Xi^A_{k+1}$.

Replica $A$ receives $\sigma Z^{(k)}$, with
$Z^{(k)}\sim\mathcal N(0,I_N)$ independent of $\Fil_k$. Replica $B$ receives
\[
 \Delta X^B_j=\sigma\varepsilon_{k,j}Z^{(k)}_{\pi_k(j)}.
\]
Given $\Fil_k$, the matching and sign vector are fixed. A permutation of
independent standard Gaussian coordinates preserves their joint law, and
multiplication by fixed signs preserves both their distributions and
independence. Therefore
\[
 \mathcal L(\Delta X^A\mid\Fil_k)
 =\mathcal L(\Delta X^B\mid\Fil_k)
 =\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2 I_N).
\]
Each replica has the same conditional transition as the uncoupled solver.
Induction over the stages proves equality of its complete trajectory law
with the baseline law. The joint law of the two replicas changes because
their increments are dependent. \qed

Independence of the fresh vector and predictability of the rule justify
conditioning before the draw. Bijectivity ensures independence within each
replica, and unit-modulus multipliers preserve the variance. The particular
rule \eqref{eq:switch} also needs nonzero masses. Retaining each particle's
carried velocity in the state makes the deterministic update well defined.

\subsection{Lemma~\ref{lem:wholeline}}

Fix $c\in\R$ and define the compactly supported difference of indicators
\[
 h_a(x)=\ind\{x\ge a\}-\ind\{x\ge c\}.
\]
Integrating their product over the overlapping intervals gives
\[
 \int_\R h_a(x)h_b(x)\,\mathrm dx
 =\tfrac12\bigl(|a-c|+|b-c|-|a-b|\bigr).
\]
Since each collection has zero total mass, subtracting the common indicator
at $c$ leaves either field unchanged. Thus
\[
 D_A=\sum_i m_i^A h_{X_i^A},\qquad
 D_B=\sum_j m_j^B h_{X_j^B}.
\]
Expanding the product and applying the preceding identity gives
\[
 \int_\R D_AD_B\,\mathrm dx
 =\frac12\sum_{i,j}m_i^A m_j^B
   \bigl(|X_i^A-c|+|X_j^B-c|-|X_i^A-X_j^B|\bigr).
\]
The first group vanishes because $\sum_jm_j^B=0$, and the second vanishes
because $\sum_im_i^A=0$. The remaining term is \eqref{eq:wholeline}. \qed

\subsection{Proposition~\ref{prop:finalstage}}

Condition on the pre-final state. Write $D_\bullet$ as in
Lemma~\ref{lem:wholeline} for the terminal configurations, so that
$\bar U=u_-+\tfrac12(D_A+D_B)$ and
\[
  V_\bullet=\int\Var\bar U
  =\tfrac14\Bigl(\int\Var D_A+\int\Var D_B+2\int\mathrm{Cov}(D_A,D_B)\Bigr).
\]
By Proposition~\ref{prop:law} the first two integrals do not depend on the
coupling. For the covariance, apply Lemma~\ref{lem:wholeline} to the two fields
with their coupled noise. Apply it again with independent noise between
replicas to obtain the product of their means. Independent Gaussian
displacements have difference variance $2\sigma^2$, so subtraction gives
\[
  \int\mathrm{Cov}(D_A,D_B)
  =-\tfrac12\sum_{i,j}m^A_im^B_j
     \Bigl[\E\bigl|X^A_i-X^B_j\bigr|-g_{\sigma\sqrt2}(a_i-b_j)\Bigr].
\]
For $i\ne j$ the increments $Z_i$ and $\varepsilon_jZ_j$ are independent with
variance $\sigma^2$ each under \refl{} and \swi{}, so
$X^A_i-X^B_j=(a_i-b_j)+\sigma\sqrt2\,\mathcal N(0,1)$ in law and the bracket
vanishes. Only the diagonal survives. There, under \refl{},
$X^A_i-X^B_i=d_i+2\sigma Z_i$ and the first term is $g_{2\sigma}(d_i)$. Under
\swi{} it is $g_{2\sigma}(d_i)$ when $m^A_im^B_i>0$ and, when
$m^A_im^B_i<0$, the two increments cancel exactly and the first term is
$|d_i|$. Subtracting,
\[
 \begin{aligned}
  \int\mathrm{Cov}_{\refl}-\int\mathrm{Cov}_{\swi}
  &=-\tfrac12\!\!\sum_{m^A_im^B_i<0}\!\! m^A_im^B_i
      \bigl[g_{2\sigma}(d_i)-|d_i|\bigr]\\
  &=\tfrac12\!\!\sum_{m^A_im^B_i<0}\!\!\bigl|m^A_im^B_i\bigr|
      \bigl[g_{2\sigma}(d_i)-|d_i|\bigr].
 \end{aligned}
\]
and multiplying the covariance difference by $1/2$ gives \eqref{eq:finalstage}.
Finally, Jensen's inequality gives
\[
 g_s(d)=\E|d+sZ|\ge|\E(d+sZ)|=|d|,
\]
which establishes non-negativity. \qed

\section{A two-particle monotonicity counterexample}
\label{app:counterexample}
Take $u_-=0$, $m=(1/2,-1/2)$, $h=0.005$, $\nu=10^{-10}$, and two steps.
The initial positions are $(p,0)$, with $p_L=-0.00275$ or
$p_R=-0.00225$. Both initial carried velocities are $(1/2,0)$.
Without diffusion, the first transport puts the positive particle at
$-0.00025$ in state $L$ and $+0.00025$ in state $R$. Re-sorting therefore
leaves the velocities $(1/2,0)$ in $L$ but assigns $(-1/2,0)$ to the
negative and positive particles, respectively, in $R$. After the second
transport, the particle positions in identity order are
\[
 L:(0.00225,0),\qquad R:(0.00025,-0.0025).
\]
At the probe $x_*=0.00125$, the reconstructed fields are $-1/2$ and $0$.

Now restore the Gaussian displacements with $\sigma=10^{-6}$. On the event
that each of the four displacements of a trajectory has modulus at most
$\delta=10^{-4}$, neither its second transport ordering nor its terminal
probe classification changes. The relevant ordering gap is $0.00025>2\delta$
and the smallest probe margin is $0.001>2\delta$. Hence the same field values
hold on this event. For either initial state the exceptional probability is
at most $\epsilon=8\Phi(-100)<\exp(-5003)$. Since every field value is in
$[-1/2,1/2]$,
\[
 \E U_R(x_*,2h)-\E U_L(x_*,2h)\ge\tfrac12-\tfrac32\epsilon>0.
\]
The initial movement of a positive mass to the right thus increases the
expected terminal field. This proves the claim in Remark~\ref{rem:mono}
without inferring an expectation from a finite set of simulation seeds.

\section{Test problems, initialisation and reference solutions}
\label{app:setup}

\paragraph{Initialisation.}
For smooth profiles with equal far-field values, let
\[
 w_+(x)=\max(u_0'(x),0),\qquad
 w_-(x)=\max(-u_0'(x),0).
\]
Both have integral $\mathrm{TV}(u_0)/2$. Their normalized cumulative
distributions are
\[
 F_\pm(x)=\frac{2}{\mathrm{TV}(u_0)}\int_{-\infty}^x w_\pm(y)\,\mathrm dy.
\]
For each sign class, we place $N/2$ particles at midpoint quantiles,
\[
 r_k=\frac{k-\tfrac12}{N/2},\qquad
 X^0_{k,\pm}=F_\pm^{-1}(r_k),\qquad k=1,\ldots,N/2,
\]
where $F_\pm^{-1}$ denotes the generalized inverse. The assigned masses are
$\pm\mathrm{TV}(u_0)/N$. This deterministic placement makes the initial state
identical across replicas and comparison arms. For the main two-pulse
experiments, the code approximates the cumulative integrals by the trapezoid
rule on $400\,001$ points in $[-12,12]$ and inverts them by linear
interpolation. It assigns the same absolute mass to both sign classes.

The profile/time study uses a separate numerical initialization on
$200\,001$ points in $[-6,6]$. It differentiates the sampled initial profile,
forms cumulative rectangle sums of its positive and negative parts, and
inverts each cumulative sum by linear interpolation. If $M_+$ and $M_-$
are those two numerical integrals, the assigned masses are
\[
 m_{k,+}=\frac{M_+}{N/2},\qquad
 m_{k,-}=-\frac{M_-}{N/2}.
\]
The reconstruction constant is $u_0(-6)$. This construction has equal counts
per sign class but does not enforce equality of $M_+$ and $M_-$. For the
oscillatory profile, its total signed mass is approximately
$-2.34\times10^{-7}$. The profile/time comparisons are numerical comparisons
of these discrete initial states, and do not invoke the zero-mass identity
for the complete evolution.

\paragraph{Profiles.}
The smooth initial profiles are given in \eqref{eq:twopulse} and \eqref{eq:profiles}.
The reported sign-region counts use a $40\,001$-point evaluation of $u_0'$
and a relative magnitude threshold of $10^{-6}$. This gives $2$, $3$, and
$13$ regions for the bump, two-pulse, and oscillatory profiles. The counts
refer to gradients above the numerical threshold, rather than to every zero
of the analytic derivative on the real line.

The prescribed signed-particle configurations in Section~\ref{sec:structure}
use $N$ particles. The interleaved case alternates masses $\pm1/N$ on
$[-0.25,0.25]$. The separated case places positive masses on $[-2.0,-1.4]$
and negative masses on $[1.4,2.0]$. The merging case uses the intervals
$[-0.9,-0.5]$ and $[0.5,0.9]$. In the latter two cases there are $N/2$
particles of each sign, with magnitude $1/N$. The one-sign control instead
assigns mass $-2/N$ to every particle on $[-1,1]$.

\paragraph{References.} For $f(u)=u^2/2$ and $f(u)=u^3/3$ the reference is a
Fourier pseudospectral solution on $[-16,16]$ with $M=2048$ modes,
$\tfrac23$-rule dealiasing and integrating-factor RK4 at $\mathrm dt=10^{-4}$.
For Burgers, the Cole--Hopf transformation supplies an independent
whole-line reference \citep{hopf1950,cole1951}. With
\[
 F(y)=\int_0^y u_0(z)\,\mathrm dz,
\]
the transformed heat solution and the recovered field are
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \phi(x,t)&=\frac1{\sqrt{4\pi\nu t}}
   \int_\R\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-y)^2}{4\nu t}-\frac{F(y)}{2\nu}\right)
   \,\mathrm dy,\\
 u(x,t)&=-2\nu\,\frac{\partial_x\phi(x,t)}{\phi(x,t)}.
 \end{aligned}
\]
The potential $F$ is analytic for the Gaussian pulses. A stabilized
evaluation agrees with the spectral solution to $5.57\times10^{-14}$ in
maximum norm at the reconstruction points. This is a numerical consistency
check, not a certified quadrature error bound. For the cubic flux, halving
$\mathrm dt$ and doubling $M$ changes the solution by $1.6\times10^{-13}$.
These reference calculations assess field error. They are not timed
competitors and do not enter the fit of a control coefficient.

\section{Additional estimator constructions}
\label{app:comparators}

This appendix specifies the supporting estimators used in

\subsection{Conditional evaluation and known-mean controls}

For conditional evaluation of the last diffusion stage, let $(a_i,m_i)$ be
one replica's pre-final configuration and let $\Phi$ denote the standard
normal cumulative distribution function. Then
\[
 \E\bigl[\ind\{a_i+\sigma Z_i\le x\}\mid\Fil_{K-1}\bigr]
 =\Phi\!\left(\frac{x-a_i}{\sigma}\right).
\]
The conditional field is therefore
\[
 u_-+\sum_i m_i\Phi\!\left(\frac{x-a_i}{\sigma}\right),
\]
and we average this expression over the two replicas. This operation
integrates out the final indicator noise, rather than replacing a random
displacement by zero.

The heat control and frozen-velocity control use the Brownian displacement
$W_i$ accumulated by each particle identity. Their terminal particle
positions are, respectively,
\[
 X_{i,H}=X_i^0+W_i,\qquad
 X_{i,F}=X_i^0+v_i^0T+W_i.
\]
Here $v_i^0$ is the deterministic initial rank velocity, held fixed in the
surrogate. The cumulative fields built from these positions have known means
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \mu_H(x)&=u_-+\sum_i m_i\Phi\!\left(\frac{x-X_i^0}{\sqrt{2\nu T}}\right),\\
 \mu_F(x)&=u_-+\sum_i m_i\Phi\!\left(\frac{x-X_i^0-v_i^0T}{\sqrt{2\nu T}}\right).
 \end{aligned}
\]
To obtain these means, observe that the predictable sign and permutation
transforms preserve the conditional Gaussian law of every fresh increment.
Each identity's accumulated displacement consequently has distribution
$\mathcal N(0,2\nu T)$.

Writing $\bar H$ for the average heat-control field of the two replicas gives
the control estimator
\[
 \bar U_c(x)=\bar U(x)-c\bigl(\bar H(x)-\mu_H(x)\bigr).
\]
The frozen-velocity estimator is defined in the same way. We examine $c=1$
and a fitted scalar coefficient. On a disjoint training sample, the latter is
\[
 c^*=\frac{\sum_\ell\widehat{\operatorname{Cov}}
       (\bar U(x_\ell),\bar H(x_\ell))}
      {\sum_\ell\widehat{\Var}(\bar H(x_\ell))}.
\]
This minimizes the sample integrated variance over scalar coefficients.
The common factor $\Delta x$ cancels between numerator and denominator.
We hold the fitted coefficient fixed during evaluation and report its
training cost separately.

\subsection{Randomized quasi-Monte Carlo implementation}
\label{app:rqmc}

One \texttt{scipy.stats.qmc.Sobol} engine with $d=2$ and $30$ bits is created per
replicate and scrambled once. Successive time steps consume successive blocks of
$N$ points, as in his equation~(45). SciPy's \texttt{scramble=True} is a
left-matrix scramble combined with a digital random shift, not fully nested Owen
scrambling \citep{owen1998,virtanen2020}. The first coordinate assigns a rank by
$\lfloor N p_1\rfloor$ and the second supplies the increment
$\sigma\Phi^{-1}(p_2)$, with $p_2$ clipped to $[2^{-53},1-2^{-53}]$ and the clip
count recorded. $N$ must be a power of two. We do not claim this adaptation
preserves the interacting discrete law. Its total error is measured
independently, including its own bias. The rank-bijection assertion is checked outside the timed path.


\section{Uncertainty methodology and its calibration}
\label{app:uncertainty}

\paragraph{Sample quantities and bootstrap intervals.}
For $n$ independent pair fields, let $\langle\bar U\rangle$ denote their
sample mean. The estimates corresponding to \eqref{eq:decomposition} are
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \widehat V&=\frac1{n-1}\sum_{r=1}^n
   \bigl\|\bar U^{(r)}-\langle\bar U\rangle\bigr\|_\Omega^2,\\
 \widehat{b^2}&=\bigl\|\langle\bar U\rangle-u_{\mathrm{ref}}\bigr\|_\Omega^2
   -\frac{\widehat V}{n}.
 \end{aligned}
\]
The subtraction removes the expected sampling contribution to the squared
error of the estimated mean. The resulting estimate can be negative even
though the population squared bias is nonnegative. We retain its sign in
the planning analysis rather than replacing it by zero.

To form a bootstrap interval \citep{efron1981}, we draw $n$ realization
indices with replacement and recompute the complete statistic, including
both parts of any variance ratio. A paired comparison uses the same indices
for its arms. Independent arms are resampled independently. The reported
percentile interval consists of the $2.5$th and $97.5$th percentiles of the
resampled statistic, with resample counts given in Section~\ref{sec:setup}.

\paragraph{Intervals for held-out error.}
Let $e_r$ be the integrated squared error of the complete estimator in block
$r$, for $r=1,\ldots,32$. We report the mean $\bar e$ and use the upper end
of a Bonferroni-adjusted two-sided $t$ interval,
\[
 U=\bar e+t_{31,\,1-\alpha/(2J)}\frac{s_e}{\sqrt{32}},
 \qquad \alpha=0.05,
\]
where $s_e$ is the sample standard deviation of the block errors and $J$
is the number of arms in that experiment. Comparing $U$ with the target
assesses expected error under this interval approximation. It does not
give the probability that an individual execution falls below the target.

\paragraph{Scalar variance-ratio calibration.}
The two interval procedures used for scalar observables are a whole-replicate
percentile bootstrap and $F(n-1,n-1)$. The second assumes independent normal
replicates, which we do not assert, so we calibrated both.

Per-replicate values of four observables were taken from the archived $N=8192$
fields, separately for the reflected and sign-switched arms, and each
sample was centred and scaled by its own standard deviation to give two
empirical shapes. A synthetic numerator arm resamples the sign-switched shape,
scaled to impose a known variance ratio $r$. The denominator arm resamples the
reflected shape. Nominal-$95\%$ intervals are then formed by each procedure and
coverage counted over $M=1500$ synthetic datasets, with $n=48$ per arm and $400$
bootstrap resamples per interval. Table~\ref{tab:calibration} reports the
two-shape design together with the common-shape design of an earlier
calibration, which used one standardised sample for both arms and therefore
does not retain the observed difference between the two distributions.
